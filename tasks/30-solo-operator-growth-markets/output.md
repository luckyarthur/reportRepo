# Where the World Market Is Growing — and Which Parts One Person Can Actually Enter

**Coverage:** Global growth by region and structural shift as of the research date; 20 solo-accessible opportunities screened and scored; the arithmetic and base rates behind financial independence.
**Research performed:** 2026-09-11. **All figures USD unless stated, nominal, point-in-time.** Market forecasts are estimates, not facts.

> **This is educational research. It is not financial, tax, legal, or investment advice.** It contains no income promises. Most solo ventures do not reach a full-time income — the base rates are in §8 and you should read that section before any of the profiles.

---

## 1. Method, evidence tiers, and the screen

### Evidence tiers
- **(A) Primary/official** — national statistical offices (BLS, Census, Eurostat, ONS), IMF/World Bank/OECD, SEC filings, platform-published transparency reports, regulator disclosures.
- **(B) Independent** — peer-reviewed research, NBER papers, reputable trade press with its own reporting, data providers with published methodology.
- **(C) Reported/promotional** — commercial market-research press releases, vendor blogs, course sellers, income-report posts, "top 10 side hustle" listicles, and any figure whose original source cannot be traced.

**Rule applied throughout: no opportunity is recommended on tier (C) evidence alone.** Tier-(C)-only candidates are quarantined in §11.

### Access constraint — stated plainly
This report was produced under a **partial web-access constraint**. Web *search* was available and used extensively; **direct page fetching was blocked by this environment's network egress policy** for every external host attempted (`imf.org`, `bls.gov`, `fred.stlouisfed.org` and others all returned `EGRESS_BLOCKED`).

**Consequence:** figures below were verified against search-index summaries of primary sources, **not by opening those sources**. Each is linked for one-click verification. Where a number rests on a single source, it is marked **[single-source]**. Several operational estimates in the profiles (startup capital, time to revenue) are **author's estimates from the cited rate and cost data** and are labelled as such — they are reasoning, not measurement.

### The solo-accessibility screen
An opportunity qualifies only if **all** hold:
1. **Startup capital under ~$10,000** (actual figure stated per opportunity);
2. **No employees required** to reach meaningful revenue (contractors and software permitted);
3. **No licence or credential requiring more than ~12 months** — or, where one exists, its cost and duration stated;
4. **No outside investment required**;
5. **Legally open to an individual** in most jurisdictions.

## 2. TL;DR

1. **Global growth is unremarkable — roughly 3.1–3.3% for 2026** (IMF), with advanced economies at ~1.8% and the US at ~2.4%. There is no broad rising tide. Everything interesting is in the composition, not the aggregate (A, [IMF WEO Jan 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/01/19/world-economic-outlook-update-january-2026), [April 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/04/14/world-economic-outlook-april-2026)).
2. **The honest headline of this report: most of the fastest-growing sectors are closed to individuals.** Semiconductors, grid-scale energy, pharmaceuticals, defence primes, frontier AI — all growing fast, all requiring capital and credentials an individual does not have. The growth-vs-accessibility matrix in §4 exists to make this visible rather than to hide it.
3. **Where growth and accessibility do overlap, it is usually at the service layer of a capital-intensive boom** — not the boom itself. You cannot build a data centre; you can be the electrician wiring it, the compliance consultant certifying it, or the technical writer documenting it.
4. **The single best-evidenced opportunity in this report is the skilled trades**, and it is the one the "financial freedom" genre never mentions: an estimated **2.1 million** unfilled skilled-trade positions projected by 2030, US construction alone needing **530,000+ additional workers in 2026**, and certified HVAC technicians reportedly down ~50% over a decade while demand rose ([JLL](https://www.jll.com/en-us/newsroom/critical-skilled-trades-shortage-threatens-economic-losses), [Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2026/03/20/skilled-trade-demand-randstand-report-electricans-technicans-construction-workers-six-figure-salaries-data-center-boom), B).
5. **B2B beats consumer for solo operators, almost always.** Higher prices, fewer customers needed, less marketing spend, more durable relationships. AI-implementation consulting for SMBs runs **$75–$150/hr for independents**, with typical SMB projects at **$10,000–$15,000** ([AI Essentials](https://aiessentials.us/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-hire-an-ai-consultant-for-my-small), [Layer3Labs](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/ai-consulting-rates-pricing), B/C).
6. **The creator economy is a lottery with a long tail, and the platform data says so.** The median Patreon creator earns **under $100/month**; fewer than **2%** clear $1,000/month. On Substack the median paid writer takes home around **$4,000/year**, and nearly half of paid creators made under **$500 across a full year** ([Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/patreon-users), [Earnly](https://earnly.ai/blog/how-much-do-substack-writers-make), C/B).
7. **Dropshipping is not recommended and the evidence is why.** Estimated failure rates of **70–90%**, with some sources at 95% within year one, and no tier-(A) or (B) data anywhere — an entire category whose numbers come from the people selling courses about it ([DoDropshipping](https://dodropshipping.com/dropshipping-statistics/), [TrueProfit](https://trueprofit.io/blog/dropshipping-success-rate), C).
8. **Amazon FBA has a real, published distribution and it is instructive**: average seller revenue ~**$160,000**/yr but **median ~$35,000**, with **13% unprofitable**. The gap between mean and median is the whole story of this genre ([TrueProfit](https://trueprofit.io/blog/amazon-fba-success-rate), C/B).
9. **Trading is excluded by rule, and the regulator's own numbers are the reason**: ESMA data shows **74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose money**, which is why brokers in the EU/UK are legally required to print their own loss percentage on their homepage ([Wolf Street](https://wolfstreet.com/2020/07/28/60-80-of-retail-traders-lost-money-with-forex-cfds-which-are-illegal-in-the-us-but-not-in-the-eu-uk-australia-other-countries/), [Babypips](https://www.babypips.com/news/almost-80-percent-of-retail-traders-are-unprofitable), A/B).
10. **Base rate for anything you start: about 22% of new US businesses close within one year, ~49% within five, ~65% within ten** (BLS Business Employment Dynamics, A via [LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/business/small/failure-rate/), [Commerce Institute](https://www.commerceinstitute.com/business-failure-rate/)). These are *all* businesses, including well-capitalised ones with employees. A solo venture with under $10k is in the harder half of that distribution.
11. **"Financial freedom" has a number and you should compute it before choosing anything.** Morningstar's base-case safe withdrawal rate for 2026 retirees is **3.9%** (up from 3.7% for 2025), implying roughly **26× annual spending** in invested capital; a flexible "guardrails" approach supports up to ~5.7% ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/whats-safe-retirement-withdrawal-rate-2026), [FA-Mag](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/morningstar-safe-retirement-withdrawal-rate-for-2026-is-3-9-85940.html), B).
12. **Don't do this:** don't quit a salaried job in a growing field to chase a solo venture with worse risk-adjusted expected value — for many readers the correct answer is a better job plus a high savings rate, and that option is systematically absent from the genre.
13. **Don't do this:** don't buy a course to learn a business model whose only evidence comes from course sellers. The reflexivity test in §9 catches most of these in under a minute.
14. **Don't do this:** don't build anything before you have sold it once. Every profile in §6 ends with a cheap falsifiable test for exactly this reason.

## 3. The macro map

### 3.1 Growth by region, as of the research date

| Region / economy | 2026 projection | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **World** | **3.1–3.3%** | January 2026 WEO Update: 3.3%; April 2026 WEO: 3.1%, revised down on geopolitical tension |
| Advanced economies | ~1.8% | 1.7% projected for 2027 |
| United States | ~2.4% | Supported by fiscal policy and a lower policy rate |
| Emerging markets | "just above recent averages" | Easing trade tension, resilient domestic demand in parts |
| Middle East & Central Asia | 3.7% (2025) → **3.9%** (2026) → 4.0% (2027) | One of the clearer accelerations |

Source: [IMF WEO Update, January 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/01/19/world-economic-outlook-update-january-2026) and [WEO April 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/04/14/world-economic-outlook-april-2026) (A, via search summary).

**Read this correctly.** A world growing at ~3% is a world where *aggregate* opportunity is ordinary. Two useful implications: growth differentials between sectors dwarf differentials between regions, so sector selection matters more than geography for a solo operator; and the IMF explicitly notes that **AI and technology investment is concentrated in North America and Asia**, which tells you where the derived service demand will be densest.

### 3.2 The structural shifts worth naming

| Shift | The evidence anchor | Where an individual can stand |
|---|---|---|
| **AI adoption across ordinary firms** | McKinsey: **78% of companies** use AI in at least one business function (B/C, via [AI Essentials](https://aiessentials.us/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-hire-an-ai-consultant-for-my-small)) | The implementation gap: adoption ≫ competence. This is the single largest solo-accessible services opening in this report |
| **Electrification and the data-centre build-out** | **530,000+** additional US construction workers needed in 2026; data-centre construction labour shortage reported near **499,000** workers ([iRecruit](https://www.irecruit.co/insights/data-center-construction-labor-market-report), B/C) | Trades, commissioning, maintenance — not the capex itself |
| **Demographic ageing** | Retirements are the primary driver of the trades shortage; care demand rises mechanically with the age structure | Home care, elder services, and the trades vacated by retirees |
| **Reshoring / supply-chain rewiring** | IMF cites trade barriers as a live factor in 2026 forecasts (A) | Compliance, customs, sourcing, industrial services |
| **Regulatory expansion** | EU AI Act, accessibility directives, SOC 2 as a de facto B2B requirement | Certification and compliance support — boring, mandatory, under-supplied |
| **EM consumer growth** | Middle East & Central Asia accelerating to ~4%; India/Indonesia/Vietnam structurally faster than AEs | Cross-border services, localisation, regional commerce |
| **Defence and security spending** | "Global economy in the shadow of war" is the IMF's own April 2026 title (A) | Largely closed to individuals — noted for completeness, not recommended |
| **Continued shift of commerce and media online** | Ongoing; no longer a frontier | Saturated at the consumer layer; still open at the B2B tooling layer |

### 3.3 Where growth is *not*

Avoid entering now, on the evidence: **generic content production** (AI has collapsed the price floor for undifferentiated text and stock imagery); **basic translation** (same, though certified/regulated translation survives — see §6.14); **low-end graphic design and basic web build** (commoditised from both ends); **generic virtual assistance** (price-compressed by global supply plus tooling); **retail arbitrage in commodity goods** (margin compressed by platform fees and algorithmic repricing); and **any business whose entire value is knowing a tool** that is now one prompt away.

## 4. The growth-vs-accessibility matrix

The central device of this report. Sectors are placed by **measured growth** against **barrier to a solo entrant** (capital, credential, licensing, scale requirement).

| | **Low barrier (<$10k, no credential)** | **Medium barrier ($10k–$100k or 1–3 yr credential)** | **High barrier (capital- or licence-intensive)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High growth** | AI implementation services · compliance & certification support · AI data/evaluation work · B2B micro-SaaS · specialised recruiting (trades/health) | Skilled trades (apprenticeship) · EV/solar service work · home care agency · equipment-based industrial services | **Semiconductors · frontier AI labs · grid-scale energy · pharma · defence primes · data-centre development** |
| **Moderate growth** | Fractional bookkeeping/finance ops · technical & regulatory writing · cross-border professional services · niche B2B media | Private-label e-commerce · specialty food · self-storage/vending · short-term-rental management | Utilities · telecoms · commercial real estate · logistics networks |
| **Low / declining growth** | Generic content · basic translation · commodity VA work · retail arbitrage | Traditional print media · commodity retail | Legacy manufacturing · fossil extraction |

**The finding, stated plainly:** the high-growth/high-barrier cell is the largest and the most economically significant. The genre's central dishonesty is to point at that cell's growth rate and sell you something from the top-left cell as though it were the same opportunity.

**The genuinely useful corollary:** the top-left cell is populated mostly by *services to the high-barrier boom*. That is not a consolation prize — derived demand is often more accessible, faster to cash, and less capital-hungry than the primary activity, and it is where a single person can realistically stand.

### 6.1 AI implementation and automation consulting for SMBs

**What it is.** Small and mid-size businesses have bought AI tools and cannot operationalise them. The work is unglamorous: map a process, identify the two or three steps worth automating, wire the tools together, write the runbook, train the staff, stay on retainer.

**Market evidence.** McKinsey's State of AI reports **78% of companies** using AI in at least one function (B/C) — adoption is no longer the constraint; competence is. **Rates:** independent freelancers **$75–$150/hr**; specialists in agents/RAG/generative pipelines **$250–$350/hr**; Big Four **$400–$1,200+**. **Typical SMB project $10,000–$15,000**; readiness assessments from ~$2,000; retainers $5,000–$30,000/month at the higher end ([AI Essentials](https://aiessentials.us/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-hire-an-ai-consultant-for-my-small), [Layer3Labs](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/ai-consulting-rates-pricing), [Jahanzaib](https://www.jahanzaib.ai/blog/ai-automation-consultant-hourly-rate), B/C — note these are practitioner-published rate cards, not survey data).

**Economics.** Capital under $1,000 (subscriptions, a laptop). Gross margin ~95%; the cost is your time. Two projects a month at $10k is $240k/yr gross at full utilisation — **which nobody achieves**; realistic first-year utilisation is 40–60%, so **$60k–$140k is the honest first-year band** (author's estimate).

**Income distribution.** **No reliable distribution data exists.** Rate cards show what practitioners *ask*, not what they *earn*. Treat every figure here as an upper bound.

**What kills it.** Selling "AI" instead of an outcome. Buyers do not want AI; they want an invoice process that takes two hours instead of twenty. The second killer is one-off projects with no retainer — you rebuild the pipeline every month.

**Competition and moat.** Rising fast at the generalist end. The moat is **vertical depth**: "AI for dental practices" beats "AI consultant" because you learn the workflow once and sell it forty times.

**AI exposure.** Tailwind now, headwind in 3–5 years as tools absorb the integration layer. Plan for the work to migrate up toward process design and change management — where the defensible part already is.

**Skills and time.** 3–6 months from a technical baseline; 12+ months from zero. You need working knowledge of LLM tooling and automation platforms, plus the rarer skill of process analysis.

**Legal/tax.** Ordinary self-employment. Get professional-liability cover before touching anything that moves money. Be careful with client data: handling regulated data (health, financial) imports obligations you must understand first.

**First 90 days.** Weeks 1–3: pick one vertical you already understand. Weeks 4–6: manually automate one process for one business **free**, and document the hours saved. Weeks 7–12: use that as a case study; approach 40 similar businesses with a fixed-price $2,000 assessment.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** 40 targeted outreaches to one vertical with a specific, named process and a fixed price. **Fewer than three discovery calls → your offer is wrong, not your outreach volume. Stop and re-specify.**

**Verdict.** The best risk-adjusted opening in the digital category, *if* you go vertical immediately. Generalists will be squeezed.

### 6.2 Compliance and certification support

**What it is.** Getting small companies through SOC 2, ISO 27001, accessibility conformance (WCAG/EAA), or EU AI Act obligations. Gap assessment, policy writing, evidence collection, audit shepherding.

**Market evidence.** Demand is **regulatory, not discretionary** — the strongest kind. SOC 2 has become a de facto requirement to sell software to mid-market and enterprise buyers; the EU AI Act creates obligations on a phased timetable; accessibility requirements have expanded in both the EU and US. No reliable tier-(A)/(B) market-size figure for the *solo consulting* segment was found — see §12.

**Economics.** Capital under $2,000. Engagements commonly run five figures; readiness assessments low four figures. Margin ~95%.

**Income distribution.** **Not documented.** No published distribution exists for independent compliance consultants.

**What kills it.** Over-promising certification outcomes, and scope creep into work that should be an auditor's (you must not audit what you prepared — that independence rule is not negotiable and is the thing to understand before you start).

**Competition and moat.** **Genuinely low competition**, because the work is tedious and unglamorous and the "financial freedom" genre never mentions it. The moat is reputation with auditors and repeat referral.

**AI exposure.** Tailwind. AI drafts the policies; the value is judgement, evidence, and accountability — which is exactly what a buyer cannot delegate to a chatbot.

**Skills and time.** 6–12 months to credibility. Prior security, audit, legal, or quality-management experience shortens this dramatically. Certifications help but are not usually mandatory for consulting (verify in your jurisdiction).

**First 90 days.** Pick **one** framework. Read the standard end to end. Build a complete template pack. Take one client at a deliberately low price in exchange for a reference. Then price at market.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** offer a fixed-price gap assessment to 25 companies that plausibly need the framework. **Under two takers at a real price → the segment isn't feeling the pressure yet; pick a different framework.**

**Verdict.** **Recommended.** Boring, mandatory, under-supplied — the three-word description of a good solo business.

### 6.3 Fractional bookkeeping and finance operations

**What it is.** Monthly books, reconciliation, AR/AP, payroll coordination, and management reporting for 8–15 small businesses.

**Market evidence.** Steady, unexciting, permanent demand. Growth is ordinary; **accessibility and durability are the attraction**, not growth.

**Economics.** Capital under $2,000 (software, certifications). Typical monthly retainers per client vary widely by market; a book of 10–15 clients is a full-time income in most developed markets (author's estimate). Margin high; revenue is recurring and predictable — a genuinely rare property.

**Income distribution.** BLS reports occupational wage data for employed bookkeepers, which is a poor proxy for self-employed practice economics. **No reliable distribution for solo practices.**

**What kills it.** Underpricing and taking any client who asks. Also: doing the work of an accountant without the standing of one.

**Competition and moat.** Moderate; the moat is **niching to one industry** (dental, trades, restaurants, e-commerce), where you learn the chart of accounts and the tax quirks once.

**AI exposure.** Mixed and often overstated. Automation has been eating data entry for twenty years, and demand for bookkeepers persisted, because the value is reconciliation judgement and clean-up. Expect the price of transaction processing to fall and advisory to rise.

**Skills and time.** 3–6 months to competence with a certification; faster with accounting background.

**Legal/tax.** Licensing requirements vary by jurisdiction — **verify locally before offering anything that could be construed as tax advice or audit.**

**First 90 days.** Certify on one platform, pick one vertical, take two clients at a discount, systematise, then raise prices.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** approach 20 businesses in one vertical offering a fixed monthly price. **No signings in six weeks → wrong vertical or wrong price.**

**Verdict.** **Recommended for stability**, not for upside. Recurring revenue, low drama, real ceiling around $150k–$200k solo.

### 6.4 Technical and regulatory writing

**What it is.** Documentation, API references, regulatory submissions, safety documentation, standards-compliant manuals.

**Market evidence.** The generic half is being eaten; **the regulated half is not**, because it requires accountability and subject expertise. This split is the entire opportunity.

**Economics.** Capital under $500. Rates vary from commodity ($0.10/word, avoid) to specialist ($100–$200/hr for regulated domains). Margin ~98%.

**Income distribution.** BLS publishes technical-writer wage data for employees; freelance distribution is **not documented**.

**What kills it.** Competing on price in the commodity segment, where you are bidding against both global supply and generation tools.

**AI exposure.** **Severe headwind in general writing; protective moat in regulated writing** — medical devices, pharma, aviation, financial disclosure — where a human must own the document.

**First 90 days.** Choose one regulated domain. Learn its standard. Build three samples. Approach 30 firms in that domain.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** 30 targeted approaches with domain-specific samples. **No paid trial in eight weeks → your samples aren't credible to that industry.**

**Verdict.** Viable **only** in the regulated segment. Explicitly **not recommended** as general freelance writing.

### 6.5 B2B micro-SaaS

**What it is.** One small piece of software solving one narrow problem for one industry, sold on subscription.

**Economics.** $1,000–$5,000 first year (hosting, tooling, incorporation). Gross margins 80–90%. The real cost is 6–18 months of unpaid building — an opportunity cost that dwarfs the cash cost and that the genre systematically ignores.

**Income distribution.** Public indie-hacker revenue data is **self-selected and self-reported** (tier C). The visible distribution is extremely skewed; most products never reach $1,000 MRR. Treat any "$10k MRR in six months" account as a marketing artefact.

**What kills it.** Building before selling. The failure mode is nearly universal and entirely avoidable.

**AI exposure.** Tailwind on build speed — which is also a headwind, since your competitors build faster too. **Distribution, not code, is now the moat.** This is the single most important shift in this category in the last three years.

**First 90 days.** Do **not** write code in month one. Find 20 people with the problem, get 5 to describe their current workaround, get 3 to pre-pay.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** three pre-payments before any code. **No pre-payments → do not build.** This test has saved more money than any other item in this report.

**Verdict.** High ceiling, high variance, **long unpaid runway**. Only with an existing technical skill and 12 months of runway — see `tasks/27-startup-building-speechify-like-app/` for a worked product example.

### 6.6 AI data work: labelling, evaluation, red-teaming

**What it is.** Producing and grading training and evaluation data; adversarially probing models; domain-expert annotation.

**Market evidence.** Demand grew with model development; expert-domain annotation (medicine, law, code, specific languages) pays multiples of generic labelling. Reliable market-size data for the *individual contractor* segment is **not published**.

**Economics.** Capital ~$0. Paid hourly or per task; **genuinely accessible from lower-income countries**, which is rare in this report.

**Income distribution.** **Not documented.** Platform rates are visible; realised earnings are not.

**What kills it.** It is a job, not a business — no compounding, no equity, income capped by hours. The second killer is platform dependency: rates and volumes change without notice.

**AI exposure.** Self-limiting in an interesting way: the work exists *because* models need human judgement, and shifts toward harder expert domains as models improve. Generic labelling is already commoditised.

**First 90 days.** Qualify on two or three platforms; identify your expert domain; move up-market as fast as possible.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** two weeks of work to measure actual effective hourly rate after unpaid task time. **Below your local alternative wage → stop.**

**Verdict.** **Useful as a bridge** — cash while building something else. **Not a path to financial independence**, and it should not be sold as one.

### 6.7 Specialised recruiting

**What it is.** Contingency placement in one narrow, supply-constrained field — trades, nursing, specialised engineering.

**Market evidence.** This is a direct derivative of the trades shortage in §6.8 and the strongest thing about it: **2.1 million** projected unfilled skilled positions by 2030 (B). Placement fees are typically a percentage of first-year salary.

**Economics.** Capital under $3,000. Long first cycle — 2–5 months to a first placement — then lumpy but large payments. Margin ~90%.

**Income distribution.** Recruiting income is highly skewed; **no reliable solo distribution found.**

**What kills it.** Cash-flow gap before the first placement, and competing in a field with abundant candidates (which is why the narrow, supply-constrained niche is load-bearing).

**AI exposure.** Mixed — sourcing automates, relationships and closing do not.

**First 90 days.** Pick one trade and one metro. Build a candidate pool first; employers are easier to find than candidates in a shortage market. That ordering is the whole trick.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** get five qualified candidates and take them to ten employers. **No fee agreement signed → your niche is not actually short of labour.**

**Verdict.** **Recommended** for people who can do sustained outreach. High income, zero capital, real cash-flow risk in months 1–5.

### 6.8 Skilled trades — electrical, HVAC, plumbing

**What it is.** Licensed trade work, eventually as a one-person operation. Named explicitly because it is the best-evidenced opportunity in this report and the one the genre ignores.

**Market evidence — the strongest in this document.**
- ~**2.1 million** skilled-trade positions projected unfilled by 2030 ([JLL](https://www.jll.com/en-us/newsroom/critical-skilled-trades-shortage-threatens-economic-losses), B).
- US construction needs **530,000+** additional workers in 2026 ([Metaintro](https://www.metaintro.com/blog/skilled-trade-jobs-construction-electrical-plumbing-careers-2026), C/B).
- Certified HVAC technicians reportedly **fell ~50% over a decade** while demand rose ([HireAligned](https://hirealigned.com/learn/hvac-technician-shortage-statistics), C/B).
- Data-centre construction alone reports a shortfall near **499,000** workers ([iRecruit](https://www.irecruit.co/insights/data-center-construction-labor-market-report), C/B).
- **Employed** median pay: electricians ~**$62,000**; HVAC/R mechanics and installers ~**$59,810** (BLS-derived, A/B). Experienced workers $80,000–$100,000+; specialised data-centre and industrial work commands premiums ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2026/03/20/skilled-trade-demand-randstand-report-electricans-technicans-construction-workers-six-figure-salaries-data-center-boom), B).

**The screen violation, stated openly.** Licensure typically takes **2–5 years** of apprenticeship, exceeding this report's 12-month rule. It is included anyway because the evidence quality is in a different class from everything else here — but **you must know that going in**. It is the slowest entry and the most durable outcome.

**Economics.** Apprenticeship is **paid** — you earn while qualifying, which inverts the usual capital problem. Independent operation needs $5,000–$10,000 in tools plus a vehicle and insurance. Solo contractors in demand markets commonly reach $100,000–$250,000+ (author's estimate from the wage and shortage data).

**Income distribution.** **The best-documented in this report** — BLS publishes full occupational wage distributions by percentile and metro for employees. Self-employed contractor distributions are thinner, but the employed floor is a real, verifiable floor, which nothing else in this report has.

**What kills it.** Physical injury (real and career-ending), poor pricing on jobs, and cash-flow mismanagement between invoices.

**Competition and moat.** **Low competition and structurally falling.** The moat is licensure — legally enforced scarcity, which is the strongest kind.

**AI exposure.** **Immune**, and one of the few categories where that can be said without qualification. Nothing you can type replaces someone who can pull cable in a ceiling.

**First 90 days.** Apply to apprenticeships; talk to three working contractors about their actual economics; verify your jurisdiction's licensing path and cost.
**Cheap falsifiable test:** two weeks shadowing a working tradesperson. **If you hate the physical reality of the work, stop now** — no income figure survives five years of doing something you cannot stand.

**Verdict.** **Strongest recommendation in this report on the evidence**, with the honest caveat that it is a 2–5 year entry and physically demanding. If you are young, or willing to retrain, this is the highest-probability path to a six-figure solo income documented here.

### 6.9 EV charging and solar ancillary services

**What it is.** Installation, commissioning, inspection, and maintenance of chargers and small solar/storage systems — plus the certification and paperwork layer around them.

**Market evidence.** A derivative of electrification: the same labour shortage, with an additional certification premium. Specific solo-segment market data was **not verified** — see §12.

**Economics.** $3,000–$10,000 in tools and certification. Requires electrical licensing in most jurisdictions (see §6.8).

**What kills it.** Subsidy dependence — the sector's demand has historically moved with policy, and policy changes. Diversify across both installation and the more stable maintenance stream.

**AI exposure.** Immune.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** get one certification, subcontract to an existing installer for a month, and measure realised hourly earnings before buying equipment.

**Verdict.** Good adjacency **for someone already in the electrical trade**. Not a standalone entry point.

### 6.10 Home care and elder services

**What it is.** Non-medical support for older adults — companionship, errands, transport, household help — as a sole operator.

**Market evidence.** Demand is demographic and therefore among the most predictable in this report. Ageing is the same force driving the trades shortage, seen from the other side.

**Economics.** $1,000–$5,000 (insurance, background checks, any required registration). Paid hourly; ceiling limited by hours, which is the honest constraint. Scaling past a solo income requires employees — which breaks this report's screen.

**Income distribution.** BLS publishes home-health and personal-care aide wages for employees; **private-pay solo operators earn materially more per hour than agency employees**, but that differential is not systematically documented.

**What kills it.** Burnout, and the hours ceiling. This is emotionally demanding work.

**Legal.** **Licensing and registration vary enormously by jurisdiction** — some require agency licensure for any paid care. Verify before advertising.

**AI exposure.** Immune.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** one month of part-time private-pay work. **Measure how you feel at the end of it, not just the money.**

**Verdict.** Recommended for the right person: durable, immune to displacement, capped income, real human cost.

### 6.11 Equipment-based niche services

**What it is.** Buying one specialised machine and selling its output — water-damage restoration, industrial/commercial cleaning, pressure and surface work, specialised floor care.

**Economics.** $8,000–$10,000 — the top of this report's screen, and the only category where equipment is the binding constraint. The equipment is also the moat: it deters the casual entrant.

**Income distribution.** **Not documented.** Estimates circulating online are tier (C) and should be treated as marketing.

**What kills it.** Buying equipment before securing demand. **Sell the service first, rent the machine for the first jobs, buy only after repeat demand exists.** This single sequencing rule is most of the risk management in this category.

**Competition and moat.** Low — the work is unglamorous and requires physical presence.

**AI exposure.** Immune.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** sell three jobs using **rented** equipment. **Can't sell three → don't buy the machine.**

**Verdict.** Recommended, with the rent-before-buy rule treated as mandatory rather than advisory.

### 6.12 Cross-border professional services

**What it is.** Selling professional services (development, design, finance, analysis, support) from a lower-cost jurisdiction to clients in a higher-cost one.

**Market evidence.** This is the mechanism that makes several other entries in this report viable from outside the US/EU. Growth is ordinary; **accessibility is the point.**

**Economics.** Capital under $1,000. The binding constraint is **not** skill — it is **payment-rail access**: whether you can be paid reliably, and what it costs you. That varies enormously by country and is the first thing to verify.

**Income distribution.** Platform rate data exists; realised earnings distributions are **not published** by the major marketplaces.

**What kills it.** Competing on price. The arbitrage is real, but racing to the bottom of it converts a viable business into a poorly-paid job.

**AI exposure.** Mixed — commodity work compresses, specialist work holds.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** verify you can receive and convert a $1,000 payment from a client in your target market, **before** doing any client work. Many people discover the constraint after delivering.

**Verdict.** Recommended as the delivery mechanism for §§6.1–6.4 rather than as a business model in itself.

### 6.13 Localisation and multilingual QA — regulated segment only

**What it is.** Post-editing, terminology management, and QA in domains where an error has legal consequences: medical, legal, financial, safety.

**Market evidence.** General translation has been severely compressed by machine translation — this is one of the clearest AI displacement effects visible in any services market. The **regulated segment survives** because accountability cannot be automated.

**AI exposure.** **Severe headwind** in general; protective in-segment. Stated bluntly because the genre still sells "translation" as an opportunity without this distinction.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** approach 20 firms in one regulated domain offering post-editing with liability. **No paid trial → the segment is already served.**

**Verdict.** Narrow and shrinking, but real for genuinely qualified bilinguals with domain expertise. **Not recommended** as general translation.

### 6.14 Niche B2B media / paid newsletter

**What it is.** A publication serving one professional niche, monetised by subscription, sponsorship, or as a lead source for consulting.

**Market evidence — read this carefully.** On Substack, **the median paid writer earns around $4,000/year**; the mean (~$16,000) is skewed by top earners; **nearly half of paid creators made under $500 across a full year**, and most writers with a paid tier earn under $1,000/month ([Earnly](https://earnly.ai/blog/how-much-do-substack-writers-make), [Artha](https://artha.link/blog/how-much-do-substack-writers-make/), C/B).

**Economics.** Capital under $1,000. **Time to meaningful revenue: 12–24 months.** B2B niches monetise several times better than consumer topics at the same audience size, which is the only reason this entry survives the screen.

**What kills it.** Choosing a consumer topic, and quitting at month 8 — before the compounding that makes the model work.

**AI exposure.** Headwind on commodity summary content; tailwind for original reporting and proprietary data, which AI cannot generate.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** publish weekly for 12 weeks and require 100 genuine subscribers in a professional niche. **Under 100 → the niche doesn't want this from you.**

**Verdict.** Viable as a **lead generator for consulting** (§§6.1–6.3). Weak as a standalone income. The distribution data does not support treating it as a primary plan.

### 6.15 Resale and recommerce

**What it is.** Sourcing undervalued goods in a category you know deeply and reselling them.

**Economics.** $2,000–$10,000 **working** capital (recoverable, unlike fixed cost). Margin depends entirely on sourcing skill.

**Income distribution.** Not published by platforms. See `tasks/26-whatnot-platform-research/` for a detailed treatment of one live-selling platform's seller economics and earnings distribution — **not repeated here.**

**What kills it.** Generalist sourcing. Expertise in one narrow category is the entire edge.

**AI exposure.** Neutral; pricing tools have compressed the easiest arbitrage.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** buy and sell ten items in one category. **Compute realised hourly rate including sourcing time** — this is where most people discover the business pays below minimum wage.

**Verdict.** Viable, capped, working-capital-hungry, and only in a category you genuinely know.

### 6.16 Private-label e-commerce, including Amazon FBA

**What it is.** Sourcing or specifying a product, branding it, and selling it on a marketplace or your own storefront.

**Market evidence — the distribution matters more than the average.** Average FBA seller revenue ~**$160,000**/yr against a **median of ~$35,000**; **13% of sellers are unprofitable**; 57% report margins above 10%; ~30,000 million-dollar sellers exist against millions of accounts ([TrueProfit](https://trueprofit.io/blog/amazon-fba-success-rate), [AmzPrep](https://amzprep.com/amazon-seller-profit/), C/B — marketplace-tooling vendors, so treat as promotional-adjacent).

**Economics.** $5,000–$10,000 for a first inventory run. **Revenue is not income**: after COGS, fees, ads and returns, net margin is commonly 10–20% of revenue. A $35,000-revenue median seller is plausibly netting **$3,500–$7,000 a year**. That sentence is the most useful one in this section.

**What kills it.** Inventory risk (cash converted into goods that may not sell), rising ad costs, and platform dependency — account suspension is an existential event outside your control.

**AI exposure.** Neutral on the model; negative on competitive intensity, since listing and creative production got cheaper for everyone.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** sell 50 units of a small first order before scaling. **Can't sell 50 profitably → do not reorder.**

**Verdict.** Viable but **materially harder than advertised**, with genuine capital-loss risk. The median outcome is a side income, not a living.

### 6.17 Fractional work in your own profession

**What it is.** Selling the expertise you already have, part-time, to several organisations — fractional CFO, ops, marketing, engineering leadership, HR, quality.

**Why it leads on probability.** It is the **only entry in this report with no skill-acquisition period**. You already have the expertise, the network, and — crucially — the credibility. The first client is often a former employer or colleague.

**Economics.** Capital under $1,000. Priced on retainer; 3–5 clients is typically a full income (author's estimate).

**Income distribution.** **Not documented** for fractional work generally, but your own prior salary is a reasonable floor anchor, which no other entry offers.

**What kills it.** Non-compete and IP terms from your current employment — **check these before approaching anyone.**

**Cheap falsifiable test:** approach ten people in your existing network with a specific fractional offer. **No interest from ten warm contacts → the market doesn't want this function fractionally.**

**Verdict.** **For most readers with a professional career, this is the highest-probability path in this report**, and it is almost never mentioned in the genre because it is unglamorous and unteachable as a course.

### 6.18 Trades-adjacent field service

**What it is.** Repair and maintenance of a specific class of equipment — appliances, small engines, commercial kitchen equipment, medical or lab devices.

**Economics.** $3,000–$8,000 in tools, diagnostics and initial parts. Manufacturer training is often free or cheap and sometimes brings referral flow.

**Competition.** Low and falling, for the same demographic reason as §6.8.

**AI exposure.** Immune.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** complete ten paid jobs while employed elsewhere; measure realised hourly rate including travel and parts sourcing — travel time is where this model's economics are usually lost.

**Verdict.** Recommended. Lower ceiling than licensed trades, much faster entry.

### 6.19 Digital products and courses — with the reflexivity warning

**What it is.** Packaging expertise into a product sold repeatedly.

**The reflexivity problem, stated directly.** The largest topic within online course sales is **how to sell online courses**. When the most visible experts in a field make their money teaching the field rather than doing it, the field's published economics are marketing. Apply this test before believing any number in this category — including the ones you will find while researching it.

**Income distribution.** **Unpublished, self-reported, and heavily survivorship-biased.** Platform-wide data is not released by the major hosts. That absence is itself information.

**When it is legitimate.** When you have genuinely done the thing, at scale, and can prove it. Teaching what you practise is a real business; teaching teaching is not.

**AI exposure.** Headwind — generic instructional content has near-zero marginal cost now. Cohorts, community and accountability hold value; recorded video increasingly does not.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** pre-sell to 20 people before recording anything. **Fewer than five pre-sales → do not build the course.**

**Verdict.** Legitimate **only** as a secondary product from a demonstrated practice. **Not recommended** as a primary business, and specifically not recommended if your expertise is "making money online".

### 6.20 Paid B2B community and cohort programmes

**What it is.** A curated professional community or small cohort programme with a recurring fee.

**Economics.** Under $1,000. Recurring revenue; retention is the whole game and is harder than acquisition.

**What kills it.** Silence. A community with no activity churns fast, and keeping it alive is ongoing unpaid labour that people consistently underestimate.

**Cheap falsifiable test:** get 20 paying founding members before building any infrastructure. **Under 20 → not a community, just a mailing list.**

**Verdict.** Viable as an adjacency to consulting. Weak standalone.

## 8. The honest arithmetic of financial freedom

### 8.1 Definitions, because the word is used loosely

- **Replacement income** — cash flow covering your expenses from something other than a job.
- **Asset-based independence** — a portfolio from which you can withdraw indefinitely.
- **A job you own vs. an asset that pays you** — the distinction that matters most and gets least attention. A solo consultancy earning $200,000 is an excellent job. It stops paying the day you stop working, and it usually cannot be sold.

### 8.2 The withdrawal-rate maths

The "4% rule" comes from **William Bengen (1994)** and the **Trinity study (1998)**, which found that a ~4% initial withdrawal, inflation-adjusted, survived historical 30-year US periods. Both are backward-looking studies of one country's unusually good century — the two most important criticisms, and both are well founded.

**Current estimates are lower.** Morningstar's base case for someone retiring in **2026 is 3.9%**, up from 3.7% for 2025 on improved capital-market assumptions, for portfolios holding 30–50% equities over a 30-year horizon. Retirees willing to vary spending with market conditions ("guardrails") may support up to **~5.7%** ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/whats-safe-retirement-withdrawal-rate-2026), [FA-Mag](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/morningstar-safe-retirement-withdrawal-rate-for-2026-is-3-9-85940.html), [Boldin](https://www.boldin.com/retirement/safe-withdrawal-rate-morningstar/), B).

**What that means in capital:**

| Annual spending needed | At 3.9% (Morningstar base, 2026) | At 4.0% | At 5.7% (flexible guardrails) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $30,000 | **$769,000** | $750,000 | $526,000 |
| $50,000 | **$1,282,000** | $1,250,000 | $877,000 |
| $75,000 | **$1,923,000** | $1,875,000 | $1,316,000 |
| $100,000 | **$2,564,000** | $2,500,000 | $1,754,000 |

**Assumptions stated:** 30-year horizon, 30–50% equity allocation, US-centric return history, inflation-adjusted withdrawals, no allowance for tax (which can be substantial), and no allowance for healthcare cost shocks outside single-payer systems. **The guardrails column requires you to genuinely cut spending in bad markets.** In lower-cost countries the required capital falls proportionally with expenses — the most underrated lever in the entire calculation, and the one available to readers outside high-cost economies.

### 8.3 Two routes, compared

| | **Build a business that sells** | **Build cash flow, invest the surplus** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Equity event | Compounding |
| Timeline | 5–10 yrs if it works | 10–20 yrs at ordinary savings rates |
| Probability | Low — **most solo businesses never sell** | Higher, mechanical |
| Multiple | Small owner-operator businesses typically trade at low single-digit multiples of earnings; **a business that is entirely you usually has no buyer at all** | n/a |
| Variance | Very high | Low |
| Honest note | The sale is a lottery ticket on top of the income | Boring, works, requires discipline |

**The uncomfortable finding:** a solo business is usually *not* a saleable asset. Buyers purchase transferable cash flow — systems, staff, contracts, brand — not a person's calendar. If independence via sale is your plan, you must deliberately build transferability from year one, which means hiring and systematising, which breaks the solo model. Most readers will do better treating the business as **a high-income job that funds investing**, which is route two with better inputs.

### 8.4 Time-to-goal scenarios

Target: $50,000/year spending → **$1.28m** at 3.9%. Real (inflation-adjusted) returns assumed.

| Scenario | Net income | Savings rate | Real return | Years to target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conservative** | $60,000 | 20% ($12k/yr) | 4% | **~36 years** |
| **Moderate** | $90,000 | 35% ($31.5k/yr) | 5% | **~21 years** |
| **Optimistic** | $150,000 | 50% ($75k/yr) | 6% | **~12 years** |
| **Downside A** | $60,000 | 20% | 2% | **~48 years** |
| **Downside B** | Business fails in yr 3; 3 yrs of savings lost, restart at $60k/20%/4% | | | **~39 years + the lost capital** |
| **Downside C** | $90,000 income but 10% savings rate (lifestyle creep) | 10% | 5% | **~44 years** |

**These are arithmetic, not forecasts**, and every assumption is visible. Three observations the genre never makes. **The savings rate matters more than the return** — compare Moderate with Downside C, same income, twenty-three-year difference. **The income level matters less than the gap between income and spending.** And **Downside B is the realistic cost of a failed venture**: not just the lost capital but the lost compounding years, which is the larger number.

### 8.5 Base rates — read this before believing any profile above

- **22.1%** of new US private-sector businesses close within **one year** ([LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/business/small/failure-rate/), A/B).
- **~49%** close within **five years** (BLS 2024 data: 49.4% fail in the first five years) ([SoFi](https://www.sofi.com/learn/content/what-percentage-of-businesses-fail/), [Commerce Institute](https://www.commerceinstitute.com/business-failure-rate/), A/B).
- **~65%** are gone by **ten years** (65.3% no longer operating).
- **By industry**: agriculture/forestry ~66% survive to year five; mining and extraction ~40%; **food services ~35%** — the spread between sectors is larger than most people assume, and food services being the worst is worth remembering next time someone suggests opening a café.

Source: BLS **Business Employment Dynamics**, which tracks cohorts of firms founded in the same year. **Important caveat:** these figures cover *employer* businesses. Solo ventures with under $10,000 in capital are not well captured, and there is no reason to think they do better.

**Survivorship bias, concretely.** Every profile in §6 describes people who succeeded. For each one, a much larger number tried and stopped, and they did not write posts about it. The failures are not visible in your research *by construction* — you can only find the people still publishing. Hold this thought whenever a case study feels compelling.

**Era-specific conditions that are gone.** Cheap organic search traffic (2005–2015); app-store discovery before saturation; early social platform reach before pay-to-play; low-competition e-commerce before ad-cost inflation; cheap urban commercial rent in major cities. Many successful case studies from those windows are **not reproducible**, and their authors often do not know that.

### 8.6 What you give up

Rarely counted, always paid: employer healthcare (decisive in the US); employer pension contributions, which are free money you are declining; income stability and the ability to plan; unemployment insurance and sick pay; career capital and the promotions you do not get; and the psychological load of being solely responsible for your income.

**The opportunity cost nobody mentions.** For many readers the highest risk-adjusted return is **a better-paid job in a growing field, plus a high savings rate** — route two in §8.3, with better inputs and none of the variance. That option never appears in this genre because nobody can sell you a course about it. It deserves to be your explicit baseline: **any venture you choose should beat it, and you should be able to say why.**

## 9. Method, tools, and validation

### 9.1 How to size a market yourself
1. **Start with official statistics** — national accounts, industry employment, trade data. Free, methodologically transparent, dull.
2. **Build bottom-up**: number of potential customers × realistic price × plausible purchase frequency. Do this before reading anyone's CAGR.
3. **Cross-check top-down** against any published figure. A big divergence means someone's definition is doing the work.
4. **Interrogate the number.** Who published it? Are they selling something? Is the methodology public? What exactly is inside the market definition? What is the base year? Is the CAGR a forecast or a measurement?
5. **Ignore TAM entirely.** For a solo operator, total addressable market is irrelevant. What matters is: how many customers can *you personally* reach and serve, at what price.

**Five tells of a fake number.** A press release for a paywalled report; no methodology; a suspiciously round CAGR; a figure repeated identically across dozens of sites with no original source; and a market definition broad enough to include everything.

### 9.2 How to validate demand before building
Ranked by strength of signal:
1. **Pre-payment** — the only signal that fully counts.
2. **Signed letter of intent with a price** — strong.
3. **Someone describing a painful workaround they currently pay for** — strong.
4. **Booking a call after cold outreach** — moderate.
5. **Email signup** — weak.
6. **"That's a great idea"** — **zero.** Treat encouragement as noise.

**Deliver manually before automating.** Do the work by hand for the first five customers. You will learn what the product actually needs and you will have revenue while learning. Automate only what you have already sold.

### 9.3 Free and cheap data sources

| Source | Good for |
|---|---|
| **World Bank Open Data** | Country-level indicators, long series, free |
| **IMF WEO database** | Growth forecasts by country, twice yearly |
| **OECD.Stat** | Comparable data across developed economies |
| **UN Comtrade** | Trade flows by product and country — underrated for sourcing |
| **Eurostat / national statistics offices** | The most reliable sector data anywhere |
| **BLS** (US) | **Occupational wages by percentile and metro**, employment projections, Business Employment Dynamics survival data |
| **FRED** (St. Louis Fed) | ~800,000 economic series, free |
| **Google Trends** | Relative direction of interest only — never absolute demand |
| **Job postings** | The best free real-time demand indicator for a skill. If firms are paying salaries for it, they will pay contractors |
| **App-store / marketplace rankings** | Revealed consumer demand and competitive density |
| **Crunchbase / funding news** | Where capital thinks growth is — a lagging but informative signal |
| **Industry association reports** | Often free, often better than paid research |
| **Regulator filings** | Public-company disclosures are audited; press releases are not |

## 10. Scams and near-scams

| Type | The tell | Where it is documented |
|---|---|---|
| **MLM / network marketing** | Income requires recruiting others; you buy inventory; "compensation plan" needs a diagram | FTC business-guidance materials; multiple national regulators |
| **"Passive income" course funnels** | The instructor's demonstrable income comes from teaching the method, not using it | Apply the reflexivity test (§6.19) |
| **Dropshipping course economics** | Course sells for $997 and every success story is a student of the course | See §5 — 70–95% reported failure, no independent data |
| **Forex / CFD / crypto "education", signal groups, copy-trading** | Promises of returns; screenshots of gains; a broker referral link | **ESMA: 74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose money**; brokers legally required to display their own loss rate ([Wolf Street](https://wolfstreet.com/2020/07/28/60-80-of-retail-traders-lost-money-with-forex-cfds-which-are-illegal-in-the-us-but-not-in-the-eu-uk-australia-other-countries/), [Babypips](https://www.babypips.com/news/almost-80-percent-of-retail-traders-are-unprofitable)) |
| **Prop-firm "funded trader" challenges** | You pay a fee for an evaluation; the fee is the business model | Regulator warnings in several jurisdictions |
| **Franchise fee traps** | Large upfront fee, mandatory supplies, territory restrictions, unverifiable earnings claims | FTC Franchise Rule requires a disclosure document — **demand it and read Item 19** |
| **AI content farms** | Publish at volume, monetise on ads, ignore quality | Search-engine spam policies; revenue collapses on algorithm updates |
| **"Business in a box" / automated store schemes** | "We run it for you"; guaranteed returns; often Amazon-store automation | FTC has taken action against several such operations |
| **Fake market-research reports** | $4,000 report, no methodology, exists to sell consulting | Check whether the methodology is public. It usually isn't |

**One rule that catches most of them:** if the person teaching the method makes their money from teaching rather than doing, the method's evidence base is marketing. Ask what their business would earn if nobody ever bought the course.

**Two red flags in any earnings claim:** an average without a median, and a testimonial without a denominator.

## 12. Open questions / what could not be verified

1. **Direct primary-source verification was impossible** — every external fetch returned `EGRESS_BLOCKED`. All figures rest on search-index summaries.
2. **No income distribution exists** for: AI consulting, compliance consulting, solo bookkeeping, freelance technical writing, micro-SaaS, AI data work, solo recruiting, equipment services, cross-border services, or fractional professional work. That is **ten of twenty profiles** with no distribution data — a significant limitation, and the honest reason the "income ceiling" column is labelled as an estimate.
3. **Self-employed (non-employer) business survival rates** were not located separately. BLS BED covers employer firms; the solo segment is likely worse and is **not documented**.
4. **The 2.1m unfilled trades positions by 2030** figure traces to industry analysis (JLL), not a national statistical office. **[single-source for the precise number]**
5. **The HVAC "certified technicians fell ~50% over a decade"** claim comes from industry commentary and could not be traced to a primary series. **[single-source]**
6. **Dropshipping failure rates (70–95%)** have **no tier-(A) or (B) source whatsoever.** The range is wide because every estimate is promotional. This absence is itself the finding.
7. **Amazon FBA figures** come from marketplace-tooling vendors with a commercial interest in seller optimism. The mean/median gap is credible; the absolute levels are not independently verified.
8. **Patreon and Substack distributions** are reported by third-party analysts, not published by the platforms as audited transparency reports.
9. **Market sizes for compliance consulting, EV-service work, and equipment services** could not be verified to any credible source and are deliberately omitted rather than estimated.
10. **Payment-rail access by country** for cross-border services was not researched systematically; it is named as the binding constraint without being mapped.
11. **Tax and licensing treatment** varies by jurisdiction and was not verified for any specific country. Every "verify locally" note in §6 is a real gap, not boilerplate.
12. **The trades wage figures** (~$62,000 electricians, ~$59,810 HVAC) are BLS-derived via secondary reporting and are **employee medians, not self-employed contractor earnings.**
