# How Paper Money Is Made and Enters Circulation

*Scope: End-to-end explanation of how banknotes are ordered, designed, printed, distributed, and eventually destroyed, using the United States (Federal Reserve Board and Bureau of Engraving and Printing) as the primary worked example, with brief comparisons to the Bank of England and the Eurosystem. Research performed 1 August 2026.*

## TL;DR

- **The central bank orders; the government printer prints.** In the U.S. the Federal Reserve Board — not the Treasury and not Congress — sets the annual print order and submits it to the Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing (BEP). The order for calendar year 2026, submitted on 15 July 2025, ranges from **3.8 to 5.1 billion notes worth $108.9 billion to $139.6 billion**.
- **Most new notes are replacements, not additions.** Historically about **70 percent** of the print order simply replaces worn-out ("unfit") notes that Reserve Banks pull from circulation and destroy. The rest covers demand growth and inventory buffers.
- **New currency is pulled into circulation, not pushed.** Notes go from BEP to Federal Reserve Bank cash offices, where they sit as inventory. They only reach the public when a commercial bank or credit union *orders and pays for* them — the Fed debits the institution's reserve account and an armored carrier delivers the cash to branches and ATMs.
- **This is an asset swap, not money creation.** A bank exchanges reserves (one form of central bank money) for banknotes (another). The quantity of money in the economy does not change when a note is printed.
- **Worn notes come back and are shredded.** Reserve Bank sorters authenticate and grade notes at roughly 100,000 per hour; on average about **15 percent** of deposited notes fail fitness standards and are destroyed.
- **Money creation happens mostly through bank lending,** not printing presses. Physical currency is a small, demand-driven slice of the money supply.

## The Pathway, Step by Step

**1. The Federal Reserve Board estimates demand and issues a print order.** Each year the Board of Governors approves a print order by denomination and submits it to BEP. The order reflects the Board's estimate of *net* demand for Federal Reserve notes from domestic and international users, built from forecast inventory levels, the destruction rate of unfit notes, and trends in net payments to circulation. Because most of the order is replacement stock, it tracks wear-and-tear more closely than it tracks the economy. Orders are expressed as ranges so that Board and BEP staff can adjust denomination-level production during the year; for CY 2026 the $10 range was widened specifically to accommodate the forthcoming "Catalyst" $10 design.

**2. BEP prints the notes.** BEP manufactures all U.S. currency at two facilities, in Washington, D.C. and Fort Worth, Texas. Its currency operations are reimbursed by the Federal Reserve rather than funded by tax appropriations — the Reserve Banks bear the cost of the notes they order.

**3. Finished notes ship to Federal Reserve Bank cash offices.** Notes are packaged into straps, bundles, and shrink-wrapped "bricks" and shipped under security to Reserve Bank vaults. At this point they exist physically but are **not** in circulation: unissued currency in Federal Reserve vaults is excluded from currency-in-circulation figures and has no economic effect.

**4. Legal issuance.** Under Section 16 of the Federal Reserve Act (codified at 12 U.S.C. §§ 411–415), Federal Reserve notes are **obligations of the United States**, issued at the discretion of the Board of Governors through the Reserve Banks, and a Reserve Bank must pledge eligible collateral — Treasury securities, gold certificates, SDR certificates, and other Reserve Bank assets — equal to the notes it issues.

**5. Depository institutions order cash — the actual entry point.** Commercial banks, credit unions, and thrifts order currency from Reserve Bank cash offices through FedCash Services when their vault cash runs low. When an order is released to an armored carrier, the Fed **debits the institution's Federal Reserve account** for the face value. The bank has received no gift or subsidy; it has swapped one asset (reserve balances) for another (notes) at par. A bank with surplus cash runs the transaction in reverse and is credited.

**6. The public withdraws it.** The bank distributes cash to branches, ATMs, and business customers. A note enters circulation the moment a customer withdraws it — which means **the public collectively determines how much cash is in circulation.** The Federal Reserve accommodates that demand; it does not decide how much cash people should hold.

**7. Return, sorting, and destruction.** Cash deposited by retailers and consumers flows back through banks to Reserve Bank cash offices, where high-speed equipment counts, authenticates, and grades it at roughly 100,000 notes per hour on a first-in, first-out basis. Suspected counterfeits go to the U.S. Secret Service. Notes failing fitness standards — soiled, limp, torn, defaced — are cancelled and shredded; on average about 15 percent of deposited notes are destroyed. Fit notes are re-issued to the next bank that orders cash. Under the Federal Reserve's methodology (revised in 2025), estimated average lifespans are roughly **7.2 years for a $1, 5.8 for a $5, 5.7 for a $10, 11.1 for a $20, 14.9 for a $50, and 24.0 years for a $100** — larger denominations last longer because they are held as a store of value rather than spent.

## Design and Security Features

Design authority is legally separate from production. The **Secretary of the Treasury holds final approval** over U.S. currency designs. Recommendations come from the **Advanced Counterfeit Deterrence (ACD) Steering Committee** — an interagency body of the Treasury, the Federal Reserve, BEP, and the U.S. Secret Service — which monitors counterfeiting threats and reproduction technology. Because the driver is counterfeit deterrence rather than aesthetics, redesign cycles track the capabilities of consumer printing and imaging equipment.

Modern U.S. notes of $5 and above combine three categories of feature:

- **Substrate features**, built into the paper itself: an embedded **watermark** matching the portrait, a **security thread** in a denomination-specific position that fluoresces a distinct color under ultraviolet light, and randomly distributed red and blue **security fibers**.
- **Ink and print features**: **color-shifting ink** on the denomination numeral; **microprinting** too fine to reproduce cleanly on consumer equipment; and the distinctive **raised feel of intaglio printing**.
- **Optically variable devices**: the redesigned $100 note carries a woven **3-D security ribbon** whose images appear to move when the note is tilted.

The $1 and $2 notes have not been redesigned in this program and lack watermarks and security threads — the counterfeiting return on those denominations is too low to justify the cost.

## Printing Process

**Substrate.** U.S. currency is not printed on wood-pulp paper. It is **75 percent cotton and 25 percent linen**, produced exclusively for BEP by Crane Currency in Dalton, Massachusetts; possession of the paper by anyone else is illegal. Watermarks and security threads are incorporated during papermaking, not printing.

**Three printing stages, in order:**

1. **Offset.** All denominations except the $1 and $2 receive a first pass of subtle multi-color background artwork on blank sheets.
2. **Intaglio.** Engraved plates press ink into the paper under high pressure, producing the raised texture and fine line work that defines banknote engraving. Backs are printed in green and cure in a vault about three days; faces are then printed in black and cure a further three days.
3. **Letterpress.** The final pass, on BEP's COPE (Currency Overprinting Processing Equipment) lines, adds the two green serial numbers, the black Federal Reserve seal, the green Treasury seal, and Federal Reserve identification numbers. COPE/PAK combines rotary letterpress with automated finishing and packaging.

Sheets are then inspected, cut, banded into 100-note straps, bundled, and shrink-wrapped for shipment.

## Money Creation vs. Money Printing

This is the most commonly misunderstood part of the process. **Printing banknotes does not create money in the economic sense.** Every step above responds to demand for a particular *form* of money:

- When BEP prints a note, nothing happens to the money supply; the note is inventory.
- When a bank buys cash from the Fed, its reserve balance falls by exactly the face value. Total central bank money is unchanged.
- When a customer withdraws $200 from an ATM, their deposit balance falls by $200 and their cash holding rises by $200. Broad money is unchanged.

Money is overwhelmingly created elsewhere. As the Bank of England set out in its 2014 *Quarterly Bulletin* article *Money creation in the modern economy* (McLeay, Radia and Thomas, 14 March 2014), **the majority of money in a modern economy is created by commercial banks making loans** — the act of lending simultaneously creates a matching deposit. Banks are not simply intermediaries lending out pre-existing savings, and they do not mechanically "multiply up" central bank reserves. The article notes that about 97 percent of money held by the UK public is bank deposits rather than currency. Central banks influence the total indirectly through interest rates and, at times, directly through asset purchases (quantitative easing) — which create reserves, not banknotes.

The U.S. figures make the same point. At 31 December 2024 there were roughly **$2.32 trillion of U.S. currency in circulation across about 55.4 billion notes** (about $2.42 trillion by October 2025), against an M2 money supply several times larger. More than 80 percent of that value sits in $100 notes, and the Federal Reserve estimates as much as half of U.S. currency by value circulates abroad — so a large share of "printing" serves foreign demand for dollars as a store of value. Phrases like "the government is printing money" almost always refer to fiscal deficits or central bank asset purchases, neither of which involves a printing press.

## Comparative Note

**Bank of England.** The UK differs in substrate and production model. Since 2016 the Bank has issued **polymer** rather than cotton-paper notes — the £5 (Churchill) on 13 September 2016, the £10 (Austen) on 14 September 2017, and the £20 (Turner) on 20 February 2020 — using De La Rue's SAFEGUARD substrate, which lasts longer, resists counterfeiting better, and permits transparent windows. Production is outsourced: De La Rue has run the Bank's Debden, Essex printing works under a managed-service contract since April 2015, extended to 2028. The ordering logic is unchanged — banks buy notes from the Bank of England as customer demand requires.

**The Eurosystem.** Under **Article 128 TFEU the ECB holds the exclusive right to authorise the issue of euro banknotes**, but issuance and production are decentralised across the national central banks (NCBs). Annual volumes combine NCB forecasts with a central ECB forecast and require Governing Council approval; the ECB then allocates denominations to particular NCBs, so a note spent in Spain may have been printed in Austria. Circulation value is apportioned by a banknote allocation key, and because notes migrate with travellers and trade, the ECB coordinates and funds bulk transfers between NCBs to prevent regional shortages and surpluses.

## Legal Authority and Counterfeiting

Federal Reserve notes are the only currency the United States currently issues. Under Section 16 of the Federal Reserve Act they are obligations of the United States, issued through the Reserve Banks against pledged collateral, and are legal tender for all debts. Because issuance is a monopoly tied to that collateral and to Federal Reserve accounting, private note production is not merely fraud against a recipient but an attack on the issuance system itself. **18 U.S.C. § 471** makes falsely making, forging, or altering any obligation of the United States with intent to defraud punishable by up to **20 years** in federal prison; the U.S. Secret Service — created in 1865 for precisely this purpose — holds primary investigative jurisdiction.

Two caveats: print orders, lifespan estimates, and circulation totals are official but revised annually, so each is dated here. Some operational details — vault locations, shipment routing, machine-readable authentication features, internal fitness thresholds — are deliberately unpublished, and no attempt has been made to guess at them.
