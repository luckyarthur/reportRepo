# The History of the Creation of Law

*Scope: Origins of law from the earliest surviving codes in Bronze Age Mesopotamia through the emergence of modern legal systems (civil, common, constitutional, and international) in the 20th century. Research performed 2 July 2026.*

## TL;DR

- **Writing made law possible.** Rulers began carving law into stone and clay in Mesopotamia around 2100 BCE — Ur-Nammu, then Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE) — turning royal custom into a public, checkable standard.
- **Greece and Rome secularized it.** Draco (c. 621 BCE), Solon (594 BCE), and Rome's Twelve Tables (451–450 BCE) shifted law from priestly memory to written code; Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (529–534 CE) preserved Roman jurisprudence for every later civil-law system.
- **Medieval Europe split it into two threads.** English common law grew out of royal courts and Magna Carta (1215); continental Europe kept building on rediscovered Roman law through canon law and the medieval universities.
- **Codification reshaped the modern world.** Napoleon's *Code civil des Français* (1804) exported a clean, rational statute-book model to most of continental Europe and Latin America; Blackstone's *Commentaries* (1765–1769) did the parallel job for the common-law tradition by systematizing it.
- **The 20th century globalized law.** Written constitutions, the UN Charter (1945), and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (10 December 1948) made law a matter of transnational, rights-based norms rather than purely sovereign command.

## Origins and Original Purpose

Law as a written institution begins in Bronze Age Mesopotamia, where city-states had grown large enough that oral custom and priestly memory could no longer resolve disputes reliably. The **Code of Ur-Nammu**, composed in Sumerian in the late 21st century BCE (c. 2100–2050 BCE), is the oldest surviving legal text; historians debate whether the code should be credited to Ur-Nammu of Ur (r. c. 2112–2095 BCE) or to his son Shulgi. Its surviving fragments contain about 57 provisions, notably replacing lex-talionis vengeance with fixed monetary compensation for many bodily injuries while retaining death for the gravest offences. The **Code of Hammurabi** (c. 1754 BCE), inscribed on the black basalt stele now in the Louvre, contains 282 provisions covering family life, slavery, property, commerce, wages, and criminal harm. Its prologue frames its purpose explicitly: to "cause justice to prevail in the land," restraining "the strong from oppressing the weak."

Why did rulers create these codes? Four purposes recur in the scholarship. First, **social order**: growing cities needed predictable rules for trade, marriage, and inheritance. Second, **legitimation of royal authority**: publishing law let a king claim to be the guarantor of justice, often as delegate of a god (Hammurabi is famously depicted receiving law from the sun god Shamash). Third, **standardization of punishment**, curbing private vengeance and blood-feud. Fourth, **protection of commerce and property**, especially critical in trading economies. Similar impulses shaped later codes such as the **Hittite laws** (c. 1650–1500 BCE), which softened Mesopotamian punishments in favour of restitution, and the **Mosaic Law** preserved in the Hebrew Bible, which fused ritual, ethical, and civil obligations. In China, early **Legalist** thinkers Shang Yang (c. 390–338 BCE) and Han Fei (d. 233 BCE) argued that publicly known, uniformly enforced statutes were the only reliable tool of governance — a claim vindicated when the Qin unified China in 221 BCE.

## Evolution Through the Ages

### Ancient (c. 2100 BCE – 500 BCE)
Law is royal and religious. It is issued from above, justified by divine sanction, and expressed as concrete case-rulings ("if a man does X, then Y") rather than abstract principle. It regulates a stable list of concerns: family, land, injury, debt, and trade.

### Classical Antiquity (c. 600 BCE – 500 CE)
Greek and Roman practice began to separate law from priesthood. In Athens, **Draco** (c. 621 BCE) produced the first written Athenian code — proverbially harsh, but a decisive shift from oral custom held by aristocrats to text accessible to all citizens. **Solon**, archon in 594 BCE, repealed most of Draco's statutes (retaining his homicide law) and reorganized Athenian society, later regarded as a foundation of the democratic legal order. In Rome, the **Twelve Tables** (451–450 BCE) responded to plebeian pressure for written rules; tradition holds that the *decemviri* who drafted them studied Greek legislation first. Over the next eight centuries Roman jurists — Gaius, Papinian, Ulpian — produced a vast case-based literature. Emperor **Justinian I** commissioned the *Corpus Juris Civilis* (529–534 CE), directed by the jurist Tribonian, to consolidate this material: the *Codex* of imperial constitutions (7 April 529, revised 16 November 534), the *Digest* (16 December 533), and the *Institutes* teaching manual (21 November 533). That corpus became the intellectual backbone of every later civil-law system.

### Medieval (c. 500 – 1500 CE)
Law fragmented and re-integrated. In Western Europe, feudal custom and localism dominated, but the church built a parallel, cross-border system: **canon law**, systematically compiled by Gratian around 1140 in his *Decretum*. The 11th- and 12th-century rediscovery of Justinian's texts at Bologna spawned the *glossators* and *commentators* who taught Roman law as a scholarly discipline across continental universities.

England took a different path. After the Norman Conquest (1066), and particularly under Henry II (r. 1154–1189), royal justices developed a body of decided cases — the **common law** — that treated similar disputes similarly across the realm, regardless of local custom. **Magna Carta**, sealed by King John at Runnymede on 15 June 1215, added a further principle: the king himself is bound by law, and free men are entitled to lawful judgment before punishment. Although Pope Innocent III annulled it within months and it was fought over for a century, its reissued forms became a cornerstone of English constitutional practice.

In the Islamic world, jurists during the 8th and 9th centuries built **Sharia** into a full jurisprudential system. Scholars including Malik ibn Anas (d. 795) and Abu Hanifa (d. 767) founded schools (*madhhabs*); al-Shafi'i's work on the "roots of jurisprudence" (*usul al-fiqh*) formalized the method — deriving rulings from Qur'an, Hadith, consensus (*ijma'*), and analogy (*qiyas*). In East Asia, the **Tang Code** (promulgated 652 CE; authoritative commentary in 653) fused Legalist administrative technique with Confucian moral hierarchy and shaped Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese law for centuries.

### Codification Era (c. 1500 – 1900)
Two parallel movements defined modern law. On the continent, Enlightenment demands for clarity, equality before the law, and rational structure produced comprehensive **codes**. The **Napoleonic Code** (*Code civil des Français*), enacted on 21 March 1804 after roughly three years of drafting by a four-jurist commission that Napoleon Bonaparte personally chaired, ran to 2,281 articles across three books (persons, property, and acquisition of property). It abolished feudal privileges, secularized civil status, and was rapidly imitated in the Low Countries, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Latin America, Louisiana, Quebec, and, in modified form, in Germany's *Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch* of 1900. In England and the United States, the parallel achievement was analytical rather than legislative: Sir **William Blackstone**'s four-volume *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (published by the Clarendon Press at Oxford between 1765 and 1769) reduced the common law to a coherent, teachable system. In America the Constitution (1787) and Bill of Rights (1791) added a further innovation — a written, higher-law charter enforceable by courts against ordinary legislation.

### Modern Era (c. 1900 – present)
Two large trends dominate. Domestically, most states adopted written constitutions with judicially enforceable rights, converging (partially) on common institutions such as separated powers and judicial review. Internationally, states built a body of law between themselves: from the Hague and Geneva Conventions (from 1864) through the League of Nations to the **United Nations Charter** (1945) and the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (adopted 10 December 1948 by UN General Assembly Resolution 217 at the Palais de Chaillot in Paris, with 48 states in favour, none against, and 8 abstentions). The UDHR is a non-binding declaration, but it seeded the binding 1966 covenants (ICCPR and ICESCR) and regional systems such as the European Convention on Human Rights (1950). Common-law and civil-law systems have partially converged: civil-law jurisdictions rely more on case law than their 19th-century self-image admitted, and common-law jurisdictions increasingly rely on statutes and codes.

## Key Events

**Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE).** Publicly erected on a stele in Babylon, the code advertised Hammurabi's authority as guarantor of justice. Its influence lies less in specific rules (many predate it in Sumerian codes) than in the *idea* of a comprehensive, publicly displayed royal law. The stele was carried off to Susa around 1155 BCE and rediscovered by French excavators in 1901.

**The Twelve Tables (451–450 BCE).** Drafted by two successive boards of *decemviri* and formally promulgated in 449 BCE, they made Roman law public and applicable to patricians and plebeians alike. Cicero reports that centuries later Roman schoolboys still memorized them; every subsequent stratum of Roman law built on their foundation.

**Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (529–534 CE).** By having Tribonian and his commissions distil more than a thousand years of Roman jurisprudence into a *Codex*, *Digest*, *Institutes*, and later *Novellae*, Justinian preserved Roman law when the Western Empire had already collapsed. Its rediscovery in 11th-century Italy launched the medieval revival of legal science and set continental Europe on the path of codified law.

**Magna Carta (15 June 1215).** Rebel barons, mediated by Archbishop Stephen Langton, forced King John to seal a charter guaranteeing baronial privileges, church liberty, and procedural protections ("no free man shall be seized or imprisoned … except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land"). Pope Innocent III annulled it that August, but reissues in 1216, 1217, and 1225 fixed its clauses in English constitutional memory and, centuries later, in American constitutional argument.

**Napoleonic Code (21 March 1804).** The first modern civil code aimed at ordinary citizens: written in relatively plain French, structured logically, and asserting equality before the law, secular marriage, freedom of contract, and secure property. Its combination of Roman-law substance with Enlightenment form made it the export template for civil-law systems worldwide.

**Universal Declaration of Human Rights (10 December 1948).** Drafted by a UN Commission chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, with major contributions from René Cassin, Charles Malik, Peng Chun Chang, and John Humphrey, the UDHR's 30 articles cover civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights. Non-binding in form, it has become the reference point for modern international human-rights law.

## Key People

**Hammurabi (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE).** King of Babylon whose promulgated code — displayed publicly on stone — established the enduring idea of the ruler as lawgiver bound to publish and enforce justice.

**Solon (c. 630–560 BCE).** Athenian statesman and archon (594 BCE) who repealed most of Draco's harsh code, cancelled debt-slavery for citizens, and reorganized political rights by wealth class rather than birth. Ancient tradition regarded him as the founder of Athenian legality.

**Justinian I (r. 527–565 CE) and Tribonian (d. c. 542 CE).** The Byzantine emperor and his chief jurist together produced the *Corpus Juris Civilis*. Tribonian led the commissions that extracted, edited, and organised the classical jurists' writings; Justinian gave the project imperial force. Without them, the classical Roman legal tradition would likely have been lost.

**King John (r. 1199–1216) and the barons at Runnymede.** John's arbitrary taxation, military failures, and abuse of feudal customs provoked baronial revolt; the resulting Great Charter did what few earlier documents had done — bind the crown itself to legal limits.

**William Blackstone (1723–1780).** Oxford jurist whose *Commentaries* (1765–1769) turned English common law into an organized body of doctrine. Widely read by American revolutionaries and 19th-century lawyers, his work shaped how the common law thinks about itself.

**Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821).** As First Consul, Napoleon chaired many drafting sessions of the *Code civil*, pushed it through, and later attached his name to it. He is not the author, but without his political will the code would not have been enacted or exported at the scale it was.

**Eleanor Roosevelt (1884–1962).** As chair of the UN Human Rights Commission (1946–1951), she brokered the compromises between Cold War blocs that made the UDHR possible, and remained its most visible public advocate.

## Common Law vs. Civil Law Today

The two dominant families of modern law trace back cleanly to the historical threads above. **Civil-law** systems (most of continental Europe, Latin America, much of Africa and East Asia) descend from Roman law through Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis*, its medieval-university revival, and codification-era statute-books culminating in the Napoleonic Code and the German BGB. Their central instrument is a comprehensive written code; judges apply the code to the facts and, at least in theory, do not "make" law. **Common-law** systems (England, Ireland, most of the Commonwealth, and — with heavy statutory overlay — the United States) descend from the English royal courts of the 12th and 13th centuries, disciplined by Magna Carta and rationalized by Blackstone. Their central instrument is the reported judicial decision: precedent binds courts vertically, and doctrine grows case by case. In practice the two families have converged: civil-law courts consult prior decisions extensively, common-law jurisdictions rely heavily on statutes and codified regulations, and both are constrained by written constitutions and international human-rights law.
