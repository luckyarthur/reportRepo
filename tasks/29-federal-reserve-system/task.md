# Task: Deep Research — The Federal Reserve System: History, Machinery, and Contested Ground

Write the result to `output.md` in this folder.

## Objective
Search wide and deep — primary law and Fed publications, central-banking scholarship, economic history, congressional and GAO material, and reputable financial press — and produce a single **reference dossier on the Federal Reserve System** that answers four things:

1. **Where it came from** — the monetary history of the United States *before* 1913, the crises that forced the question, and the specific bargain the Federal Reserve Act struck.
2. **How it actually works** — the institutional wiring (who decides what, who appoints whom, where the money comes from) and the operational mechanics of how a policy decision becomes an interest rate in the real economy.
3. **What it has done under pressure** — the episodes that reshaped it: 1929–33, the 1951 Accord, the Great Inflation and Volcker, 2008, 2020, 2023, and the post-2021 tightening cycle.
4. **What is genuinely contested** — the honest criticisms, the live policy debates, and, separately and explicitly labelled, the popular claims about the Fed that are factually wrong and why.

This is an evidence-grounded research task. Every institutional claim, date, figure, and legal citation must be accurate and sourced. Where economists disagree, present the disagreement rather than resolving it.

## Framing — the discipline of this task
The Fed is one of the most written-about and least accurately described institutions in American life. Three failure modes must be actively resisted:

1. **The institutional brochure.** Fed self-descriptions are accurate but selective; they describe intent, not always effect. Use them as primary sources for structure and mandate, not as the last word on outcomes.
2. **The conspiracy register.** A large popular literature makes specific factual claims about ownership, secrecy, and audits that are checkable and largely false — but the underlying grievances (distributional effects, accountability, regulatory capture) are real and serious. **Do not lump them together.** Check each claim on its merits, and treat the serious critique with the seriousness it deserves.
3. **False precision about causation.** "The Fed caused X" is nearly always contested. Attribute causal claims to named economists or studies, and state the counter-position.

### Evidence tiers — label every non-obvious claim
- **(A) Primary/official** — the Federal Reserve Act and amending statutes (by section), FOMC statements, minutes and transcripts, the H.4.1 and other statistical releases, the Monetary Policy Report, Board and Reserve Bank annual reports and audited financial statements, GAO and Fed OIG reports, Congressional hearing records, Treasury releases.
- **(B) Independent** — peer-reviewed economics and economic history, NBER working papers, books by credentialed historians and economists, Fed research publications where the work is refereed or clearly research-grade, reputable financial press with its own sourcing.
- **(C) Reported/advocacy** — think-tank position papers, op-eds, memoirs of participants, popular non-fiction with a thesis, and advocacy literature of any political direction.

Label memoirs by principals (Bernanke, Geithner, Volcker, Greenspan, Yellen) as tier (C) for claims about their own judgement and tier (B) only where independently corroborated.

## Scope

### Part 1 — Before the Fed (required)
- Colonial and Revolutionary money; the Continental and its collapse.
- The **First Bank of the United States** (1791–1811) and **Second Bank** (1816–1836), Hamilton vs. Jefferson, McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), the Bank War and Jackson's veto.
- The **Free Banking era** (c. 1837–1863) and its instability.
- The **National Banking Acts** (1863/1864), the national banknote system, and the structural flaw of an inelastic currency.
- The recurring panics — **1873, 1884, 1893, and above all 1907** — what actually happened, and J. P. Morgan's private-rescue role.
- The **Aldrich–Vreeland Act (1908)**, the **National Monetary Commission**, and the **1910 Jekyll Island meeting** — what is documented about it, by whom, and when it became public (this is a fact-check node: state precisely what the historical record supports).
- The legislative fight of 1912–13 and the **Federal Reserve Act, signed 23 December 1913** — and specifically what compromises produced the hybrid public/private, centralized/decentralized design.

### Part 2 — Structure and governance (required)
- The **Board of Governors** — seven governors, 14-year staggered terms, Chair and Vice Chair four-year terms, Senate confirmation, the Vice Chair for Supervision (created by Dodd-Frank, 2010), removal protections and the live legal question around them.
- The **twelve Reserve Banks** and their districts — why twelve, why those cities, what the districts' boundaries reflect, and what the Reserve Banks actually do day to day.
- **Member-bank "stock"** — what it is, what it is not, the statutory dividend, and why it does not constitute ownership in the ordinary sense. Handle this precisely; it is the single most misunderstood fact about the institution.
- Reserve Bank **boards of directors** (Class A/B/C), how presidents are selected and Board-approved, and the conflict-of-interest critique of the Class A seats.
- The **FOMC** — twelve voters: seven governors, the New York president permanently, and four of the remaining eleven presidents on rotation; the schedule; how the statement, the SEP/"dot plot", minutes, and the five-year transcript release work.
- **Funding and remittances** — the Fed is self-funded from its portfolio and operations; surplus goes to the Treasury; explain **deferred assets** and why remittances went to zero after 2022.
- **Audit and oversight** — what GAO does and does not audit and why (the 1978 restriction), the annual independent financial audit, the Fed OIG, Humphrey-Hawkins testimony, and what "Audit the Fed" bills would actually change. Be exact.

### Part 3 — Functionality: how it actually works (required, and the technical core)
- The **dual mandate** (Federal Reserve Act §2A, from the 1977 amendments and Humphrey-Hawkins 1978): maximum employment and stable prices, plus moderate long-term interest rates. The 2% PCE target adopted in 2012; the 2020 **flexible average inflation targeting** framework and the subsequent framework review — state where the framework stands as of the research date.
- **The modern operating framework** — the shift from a scarce-reserves corridor to the post-2008 **ample-reserves floor system**: IORB, the ON RRP facility, the effective federal funds rate, SOFR, the Standing Repo Facility, and the discount window's primary/secondary/seasonal credit.
- **Transmission** — walk the chain concretely: policy rate → money-market rates → the yield curve and term premia → bank lending and credit spreads → asset prices, the exchange rate, and expectations → spending, hiring, and prices. Include the lag structure and cite the evidence on it.
- **The balance sheet** — assets (Treasuries, agency MBS, repo, loans) and liabilities (notes in circulation, reserves, the TGA, ON RRP). Explain **QE and QT** mechanically, including what reserves are and the precise sense in which the Fed "creates money" — and the sense in which it does not.
- **Lender of last resort** — Bagehot's rule, the discount window and its **stigma problem**, and **§13(3)** emergency authority and how Dodd-Frank narrowed it.
- **Supervision and regulation** — which institutions the Fed supervises and which it shares with the OCC, FDIC, NCUA and the states; bank holding companies; **CCAR/stress tests**; capital and liquidity rules (Basel III, SLR, LCR); the FSOC role; and the tailoring debate after 2018 (S.2155).
- **Payments and financial infrastructure** — Fedwire Funds and Securities, the National Settlement Service, ACH, **FedNow** (launched 2023) and its adoption path, check processing, and currency and coin distribution (cross-reference `tasks/16`).
- **The international dimension** — dollar swap lines, the FIMA repo facility, the Fed's de facto role in global dollar funding markets, and the spillover critique from emerging-market central banks.
- **What the Fed does NOT do** — a short, explicit list: it does not set fiscal policy, mint coins, print the physical notes, set mortgage rates directly, control the exchange rate (that is Treasury's call), regulate securities markets, or insure deposits. Misattribution is the most common error in public discussion.

### Part 4 — The episodes (required)
Treat each with what happened, what the Fed did, what the scholarly verdict is, and what was institutionally changed afterward:
- **1929–1933** — the Great Contraction; the Friedman–Schwartz indictment versus Bernanke's credit-channel account and the gold-standard literature (Eichengreen, Temin); the Banking Acts of **1933** (Glass-Steagall, FDIC) and **1935** (which created the modern Board and FOMC).
- **1942–1951** — wartime yield pegging and the **Treasury–Fed Accord of March 1951**, the founding moment of operational independence.
- **1965–1982** — the Great Inflation; the Martin, Burns, Miller and Volcker chairmanships; the Nixon-Burns pressure question and what the tapes actually show; the **October 1979 change in operating procedure**; the 1980–82 recessions and the disinflation's cost.
- **1983–2006** — the Great Moderation, Greenspan, the 1987 crash response, the 1998 LTCM episode, and the "Greenspan put" critique.
- **2007–2010** — Bear Stearns, the Lehman decision, AIG, the alphabet-soup facilities, ZIRP, QE1–3, and the legal and political aftermath (Dodd-Frank 2010).
- **March 2020** — the COVID dash-for-cash, the Treasury-market breakdown, unlimited QE, the CARES Act-backed 13(3) facilities (PMCCF, SMCCF, MSLP, MLF) and **how little of the announced capacity was actually drawn** — a genuinely interesting result.
- **2021–2023** — the "transitory" call and why it was wrong, the pivot, the fastest tightening cycle since Volcker, and QT.
- **March 2023** — SVB, Signature, First Republic; the supervisory failure the Fed's own report (Barr review, April 2023) admitted; the **BTFP**; and the systemic-risk exception.
- **2024 to the research date** — the easing cycle, the balance-sheet endpoint, the framework review outcome, and any live legal or political challenge to the Fed's independence. Report only what is verifiable and dated.

### Part 5 — Contested ground (required, and to be handled carefully)
Split this into two clearly separated sections.

**5a. Serious critiques** — steelman each, then give the counter-argument:
- **Distributional effects** — whether asset-price inflation from QE widened wealth inequality; cite the empirical work on both sides.
- **Moral hazard and the "Fed put"** — bailouts, risk-taking, and the 2008/2020 precedents.
- **Independence vs. democratic accountability** — the case that unelected technocrats should not make distributional choices, versus the strong empirical literature linking central-bank independence to lower inflation.
- **Mandate creep** — quasi-fiscal credit allocation, the climate-scenario debate, the MBS-purchase-and-housing question.
- **Forecasting record** — the SEP's and staff's actual track record; cite evaluations.
- **Regulatory capture and the revolving door**; the Reserve Bank Class A director structure; the 2021–22 trading scandal and the resulting rules.
- **The Austrian / Rothbard / Hayek critique** and the **market monetarist / NGDP targeting** critique and the **MMT** critique — three very different objections, each stated accurately in its own terms.
- **Rules vs. discretion** — Taylor rule, forward guidance, and the credibility literature.

**5b. Common claims that are factually wrong** — a table. For each: the claim as usually stated, what is actually true, the primary source that settles it, and — importantly — what legitimate concern, if any, sits underneath it. Cover at minimum: "the Fed is a private bank owned by secret shareholders"; "the Fed has never been audited"; "the Fed prints money and pockets the profit"; "the Fed is unconstitutional / the Federal Reserve Act was passed by a rump Congress at Christmas"; "the Fed lends money to the government at interest, which is the national debt"; "the dollar has lost 96% of its value, which proves the Fed failed" (address this one carefully — the number is roughly right and the inference is the contested part); "ending the Fed would return the US to sound money". Be factual and non-condescending; the goal is a reader who can check things themselves.

### Part 6 — Comparative and forward-looking (recommended additions)
- **Comparison table** of the Fed against the **ECB, Bank of England, Bank of Japan, People's Bank of China, and one EM central bank** — mandate, governance, independence rating, balance sheet size as % of GDP, and inflation target.
- **Central-bank digital currency** — the Fed's stated position, the FedNow-vs-CBDC distinction, the 2025-era legislative posture, and international comparisons (digital euro, e-CNY). Cross-reference `tasks/22`.
- **Stablecoins and private money** — the regulatory question as it touches the Fed, and the historical parallel to Free Banking era notes.
- **Open institutional questions** — the terminal balance sheet size, the future of the floor system, the post-review framework, Treasury-market resilience, and the legal status of removal protections.
- **How to follow the Fed yourself** — a genuinely useful reader's guide: the FOMC calendar; how to read a statement (and how to diff it against the prior one); the SEP and dot plot and their limits; the H.4.1, H.8, H.6 and G.19 releases; FRED; the Beige Book; the five-year transcript release; the Monetary Policy Report; and which Reserve Bank research publications are worth reading.
- **Tiered reading list** — (i) history, (ii) institutional and mechanical, (iii) participant memoirs, (iv) critiques from several directions — each with author, title, publisher, year, and one line on *what it teaches and where it is unreliable*. Note which primary documents are freely available (FRASER, the Board's site, Gutenberg where applicable) with links.
- **Glossary** — 25–40 terms defined in one line each (IORB, ON RRP, SOFR, TGA, SLR, LCR, SEP, FAIT, QT, repo, discount window, 13(3), CCAR, FIMA, deferred asset, etc.).

## Rules & cautions
- **Accuracy over completeness.** Do not fabricate statutes, section numbers, dates, figures, quotes, book titles, publishers, or study findings. A shorter, verified report beats a padded one.
- **Cite statutes by section.** Where you say the Act requires something, name the section (e.g. §2A, §13(3), §14, §10) and confirm it against the text.
- **Never invent a number.** Balance-sheet totals, rate levels, and inflation figures must name the release and the as-of date (e.g. "H.4.1, week ended …"). If a current figure cannot be verified, write "not verified as of <date>" and carry it into the open-questions list.
- **Date everything current.** Policy rates, the balance sheet, the framework's status, pending legislation, and any personnel facts change constantly. State the as-of date inline for every such claim.
- **Distinguish law, practice, and effect.** What the statute says, what the Fed actually does, and what the economy does in response are three different claims with three different evidence standards.
- **Attribute contested causal claims** to named economists or studies with dates; never state a disputed causal claim in the report's own voice.
- **Be politically even-handed.** Critiques come from left, right, and libertarian directions and all three have serious versions. Present them in their own strongest terms. Do not editorialise about motives, and do not treat any political figure's statements about the Fed as evidence of fact.
- **Handle the conspiracy material factually, not dismissively.** Check the claim, cite the primary source, and name the legitimate concern underneath where one exists.
- **Living people:** stick to documented public and professional conduct.
- If web access is unavailable or a claim cannot be verified, say so explicitly, list what was attempted, and mark the gap — an honest gap is a correct output.
- This is educational and historical research, **not financial, legal, or investment advice**; include a brief note to that effect. Make no rate forecasts and no market predictions.

## Style
- ~5,000–7,000 words plus tables, glossary, reading list, and sources.
- Neutral, analytical, reference-grade tone — written for a reader who wants to understand the machine and then go check the primary documents themselves.
- Use tables wherever the material is comparative (timeline, governance, facilities, fact-check, international comparison, reading list).
- Explain mechanisms concretely rather than by analogy; where an analogy is used, say what it gets wrong.
- Mark uncertain or contested figures inline with their tier — e.g. "roughly 35% of GDP (B, author's calculation from H.4.1 and BEA)".
- Use clear section headings matching the structure above, and cross-reference sibling folders by path (`tasks/16-how-paper-money-is-made/`, `tasks/22-blockchain-smart-contract-market/`) where they overlap.
