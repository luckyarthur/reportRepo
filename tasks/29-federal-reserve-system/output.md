## 2. TL;DR

1. **The Fed was created to solve one specific problem** — an inelastic currency that turned ordinary business downturns into bank runs — and its hybrid public/private, centralised/decentralised design is the fossilised record of the political compromise that got it passed in 1913 (B).
2. **The structure you see today is mostly from 1935, not 1913.** The Banking Act of 1935 created the modern Board of Governors and the FOMC as we know them; the 1913 Act had a far weaker centre (A).
3. **"The Fed" is three things**: a federal agency (the Board), twelve federally-chartered corporations (the Reserve Banks), and a statutory committee (the FOMC). Most confused claims about the Fed come from collapsing the three (A).
4. **Member banks hold "stock", but it is not ownership** in any ordinary sense — it cannot be sold, traded, or borrowed against, it carries no residual claim, and its dividend is fixed by statute (A, Federal Reserve Act §5). This single fact disposes of most of the "privately owned" literature — though the *governance* concern underneath it is real and is discussed in §9a.
5. **The Fed is audited every year** by an independent public accounting firm, plus a standing Inspector General, plus GAO across most of its activities. The 1978 statutory carve-out from GAO review covers monetary-policy deliberations, foreign-central-bank transactions, and FOMC operations — that is the actual content of the "Audit the Fed" dispute (A).
6. **The operating framework changed permanently in 2008.** The Fed no longer adjusts the quantity of reserves to hit a rate; it sets administered rates (principally IORB) in an ample-reserves floor system. Nearly all pre-2008 textbook description of "how the Fed works" is now wrong (A/B).
7. **Since 2022 the Fed has run an operating loss and remitted nothing to the Treasury.** The accumulated shortfall — recorded as a *deferred asset* of roughly **-$233bn** — must be earned back before remittances resume, which recent projections put at **mid-2031 or later** ([Seeking Alpha, Sept 2026](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4943769-fed-records-3rd-consecutive-quarterly-gain-but-remittances-to-treasury-wont-resume-any-time-soon), B/C) (A for the mechanism, [CRS IN12081](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12081/IN12081.1.pdf)).
8. **Quantitative tightening ended on 1 December 2025**, with the balance sheet at roughly **$6.57tn** — only about half of the pandemic-era expansion reversed. Repo-market stress and reserve-scarcity concerns stopped runoff before reserves reached clearly "ample" levels ([SVB](https://www.svb.com/market-insights/us-treasuries/the-federal-reserve-ends-qt-key-market-liquidity-insights/), [Banking Exchange](https://www.bankingexchange.com/news-feed/item/10480-treasury-market-resilience-and-the-early-end-to-balance-sheet-runoff), B). Total assets stood at about **$6.7tn as of 2026-09-02** (A via search summary of H.4.1).
9. **The 2020 "flexible average inflation targeting" framework is gone.** The August 2025 framework review removed the makeup strategy and the "averages 2 percent over time" language, returning the Fed to flexible inflation targeting with a heavier emphasis on anchoring expectations. The 2% goal itself was not reopened ([Federal Reserve 2025 Review overview](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/guide-to-changes-in-statement-on-longer-run-goals-monetary-policy-strategy.htm), A; [ABA Banking Journal](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2025/09/the-federal-reserves-monetary-policy-framework-the-2025-review/), B).
10. **As of the research date the policy rate is 3.50–3.75%**, held at the July 2026 meeting on a **9–3 vote with three dissents in favour of a *hike*** (Presidents Hammack, Kashkari and Logan) — an unusual configuration that reflects inflation running near 3.4% CPI / 3.3% core PCE against a labour market softening mainly through a shrinking labour force ([CNBC, 2026-07-29](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/29/fed-rate-decision-july-2026.html), B).
11. **The chair changed in 2026.** Kevin Warsh was confirmed 54–45 and sworn in on **22 May 2026** as chair; Jerome Powell remained on the Board as a governor ([CNBC, 2026-05-13](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/13/kevin-warsh-wins-senate-confirmation-as-the-next-federal-reserve-chair.html), B).
12. **The legal foundation of Fed independence was tested and, provisionally, held.** In *Trump v. Cook* (decided **2026-06-29**) the Supreme Court declined to allow the removal of Governor Lisa Cook, reasoning that the Federal Reserve Act's for-cause protection entitles a governor to notice and an opportunity to respond; four justices dissented and the merits remain unresolved in the lower courts ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/29/supreme-court-lisa-cook-trump-federal-reserve.html), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/29/nx-s1-5557955/supreme-court-fed-lisa-cook), B).
13. **The most common popular claims about the Fed are checkable and mostly false** — but the grievances beneath them (distributional effects of QE, accountability, capture) are serious and supported by real research. §9 keeps those two things apart on purpose.
14. **The honest summary of the Fed's record**: it has never again permitted a 1930s-style monetary collapse, which is the thing it was rebuilt in 1935 to prevent; it presided over the Great Inflation and then ended it at enormous cost; and it has twice (2008, 2020) prevented a financial seizure by means that transferred risk to the public balance sheet and left a moral-hazard problem nobody has solved (B).

## 4. Before the Fed

### The problem the United States could not solve

From 1836 to 1913 the country had no central bank, and the record is unambiguous: recurring, severe, seasonal financial panics (B). The proximate mechanism was specific and worth understanding, because the Fed was built as its answer.

Under the National Banking Acts of 1863–64, a national bank could issue notes only against deposited US government bonds. Note supply was therefore tied to the *stock of federal debt*, not to the *demand for currency*. Currency demand, however, was violently seasonal — every autumn the harvest had to be moved and paid for, and country banks withdrew balances from New York. The supply of money could not expand to meet that demand. This is what contemporaries meant by an **inelastic currency**, and it converted ordinary seasonal stress into liquidity crises with metronomic regularity (B).

The second structural feature was the **reserve pyramid**: country banks held reserves as deposits at reserve-city banks, which held theirs in New York, where they were lent into the call-money market. A shock in New York therefore propagated downward through the entire system, and a rush for cash anywhere pulled at the same small pile of reserves (B).

### 1907 and the political tipping point

The Panic of 1907 began with a failed corner in United Copper, spread to the Knickerbocker Trust, and became a general run. There was no public institution capable of stopping it. J. P. Morgan, then 70, convened bankers in his library and allocated liquidity personally (B).

The country was rescued, and the rescue was the scandal. A private citizen had exercised what was obviously a public function. Both the progressive left (who saw dangerous concentration of financial power) and Wall Street (which wanted a reliable backstop) concluded that some institution was needed. They wanted different institutions, and the Federal Reserve Act is what their disagreement produced.

### Jekyll Island — what the record actually supports

In November 1910, six men travelled in secrecy to the Jekyll Island Club off the Georgia coast: Senator **Nelson Aldrich**; **A. Piatt Andrew**, Assistant Secretary of the Treasury; **Henry P. Davison** of J. P. Morgan & Co.; **Frank A. Vanderlip** of National City Bank; **Paul M. Warburg** of Kuhn, Loeb; and (by most accounts) **Benjamin Strong**. They used first names only and travelled as a duck-hunting party. Over roughly ten days they drafted what became the **Aldrich Plan** (B).

Four points, stated precisely, because this episode carries more freight than any other in the Fed's history:

1. **The meeting happened and it was deliberately secret.** This is not disputed. Vanderlip wrote later: "I was as secretive — indeed, as furtive — as any conspirator" (C for the characterisation, B for the fact of the meeting).
2. **It became public early.** B. C. Forbes published an account in 1916, three years after the Act passed; Vanderlip described it in his memoirs (B). It was never a suppressed secret; it was a publicised one.
3. **The Aldrich Plan was defeated.** It proposed a single National Reserve Association, largely banker-controlled, with no presidential appointments. Congress rejected it — in part *because* of its association with Aldrich and Wall Street (B).
4. **What passed in 1913 was materially different**: twelve regional banks instead of one, a presidentially-appointed Washington board on top, and Senate confirmation. The Glass–Owen framework was the Democratic answer to the Aldrich Plan, not its enactment (B).

The honest reading: bankers did draft the original blueprint in secret, and the final statute was a political rework of it under public scrutiny. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing, and the popular literature usually keeps only the first.

### The 1913 bargain

The Federal Reserve Act (signed by Woodrow Wilson, **23 December 1913**) resolved four fights by splitting the difference on each:

| Fight | Resolution in the Act |
|---|---|
| One central bank vs. none | **Twelve** regional Reserve Banks |
| Banker control vs. government control | Reserve Banks capitalised and partly directed by member banks; a presidentially-appointed Board above them |
| Government money vs. bank money | Federal Reserve Notes are obligations of the **United States**, issued through the Reserve Banks |
| Where power sits | Deliberately ambiguous — New York dominated in practice until 1935 |

That ambiguity is not a drafting flaw; it is the deal. Nearly every subsequent structural change — 1935 above all — has been a renegotiation of the same question.

## 5. Structure and governance

### 5.1 Three institutions, one name

| Body | Legal nature | Members | Appointment | Term |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Board of Governors** | Independent federal agency | 7 governors | President nominates, Senate confirms | 14 years, staggered; Chair/Vice Chair 4 years (renewable), separately confirmed |
| **Reserve Banks** (12) | Federally-chartered corporations | Each with 9 directors and a president | Presidents chosen by Class B and C directors, **approved by the Board of Governors** | 5 years |
| **FOMC** | Statutory committee (§12A) | **12 voters**: 7 governors + New York president (permanent) + 4 of the other 11 presidents on rotation | — | Rotation annual |

The rotation groups are fixed by statute: Cleveland and Chicago alternate; the remaining nine rotate in three groups. In 2026 the voting presidents alongside New York are **Cleveland, Philadelphia, Dallas and Minneapolis** — which is why the July 2026 dissents came from Hammack (Cleveland), Logan (Dallas) and Kashkari (Minneapolis) (A/B). All twelve presidents attend and speak at every meeting; only five vote.

### 5.2 The member-bank "stock" question

This is the most misunderstood fact about the institution, so it is worth stating with precision (A, Federal Reserve Act §5, §7):

- National banks **must** be members; state banks may join. Members subscribe **6% of their capital and surplus** to the stock of their district Reserve Bank, **half paid in**.
- The stock **may not be sold, traded, pledged, or transferred**. There is no market in it and no way to profit from its appreciation, because it does not appreciate.
- It carries **no residual claim on profits**. The dividend is fixed by statute — historically 6%; since the FAST Act of 2015, banks with more than $10bn in assets receive the lesser of the 10-year Treasury yield or 6%.
- Voting rights extend only to electing **six of nine** directors of the district Reserve Bank (Class A and B), and Class A directors — the bankers — are barred from the president-selection process, which is conducted by Class B and C directors and requires Board of Governors approval.
- **Residual earnings go to the Treasury**, not to members: after expenses, dividends, and maintaining a surplus capped by statute (currently **$6.825bn**), all net income is remitted to the US Treasury.

So: the stock is best understood as a **mandatory, non-transferable membership assessment with a statutory coupon**. Calling it "ownership" imports property rights that do not exist here.

**The legitimate concern underneath it** is different and real: the Class A director seats give bankers a formal role in the governance of their own supervisor, and Reserve Bank presidents — who cast five of twelve FOMC votes — are not Senate-confirmed. That is a genuine accountability argument, made by serious people, and it is discussed in §9a. It is not the same claim as "secret shareholders own the Fed", and conflating them has made the real argument harder to have.

### 5.3 Money in, money out

The Fed receives **no congressional appropriation**. It funds itself from interest on its securities portfolio, plus fees for priced services (cheque clearing, Fedwire, FedNow) charged to banks (A).

Historically this produced a large surplus remitted to the Treasury — over **$100bn in 2021** at the peak (B). Then the mechanism reversed. The Fed holds long-duration, low-coupon assets bought in 2020–21 and funds them with overnight liabilities — reserves and ON RRP balances — on which it pays the policy rate. When the policy rate rose above the portfolio's yield, net interest income went negative.

- **Remittances have been zero since September 2022** (A/B).
- Accumulated losses are booked as a **deferred asset** — an accounting entry representing future earnings the Fed must retain before it may remit again.
- That deferred asset stands near **-$233bn**, and projections cited in September 2026 put resumption of remittances at **mid-2031 or later** ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4943769-fed-records-3rd-consecutive-quarterly-gain-but-remittances-to-treasury-wont-resume-any-time-soon), C/B) **[single-source for the precise figure and date]**.

Two things are true at once and both matter. The Fed cannot become insolvent in the ordinary sense — it creates the liabilities it owes in its own unit of account — so the accounting loss does not threaten its operations. And the lost remittances are a real cost to the federal budget, running to hundreds of billions, which is a legitimate subject for criticism (see [CRS IN12081](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12081/IN12081.1.pdf)).

### 5.4 Audit and oversight — what is actually true

| Mechanism | Scope | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent financial audit** | Annual audit of Board and combined Reserve Bank financial statements by an external public accounting firm; results published in the Annual Report | Board policy (A) |
| **Inspector General** | Standing OIG for the Board and CFPB; audits, investigations, programme reviews | Inspector General Act (A) |
| **GAO** | Broad authority over Fed operations, supervision, payments, and financial management — **except** three carve-outs | Federal Banking Agency Audit Act of 1978 (A) |
| **GAO carve-outs** | (i) monetary-policy deliberations and decisions; (ii) transactions with foreign central banks and governments; (iii) FOMC open-market operations | Same statute |
| **One-time full audit** | GAO review of *all* crisis-era emergency lending, published **July 2011**, including counterparty-level detail | Dodd–Frank §1109 (A) |
| **Congressional oversight** | Semiannual Monetary Policy Report and testimony; confirmation hearings; subpoena power | Humphrey–Hawkins 1978 (A) |
| **Disclosure** | Weekly H.4.1; FOMC statement same day; minutes at 3 weeks; **full transcripts at 5 years**; discount-window borrower detail at 2 years (Dodd–Frank) | (A) |

"Audit the Fed" bills would remove the three 1978 carve-outs — principally, they would open monetary-policy deliberations to GAO review in real time. **That is the entire substantive dispute.** Supporters say no government function should be exempt from the auditor. Opponents — including every Fed chair of the modern era — argue it is a mechanism for political pressure on individual rate votes rather than an accounting measure, since the financial books are already audited. Both positions are coherent; neither depends on the Fed being unaudited, because it is not.

## 6. How it actually works

### 6.1 The mandate

Federal Reserve Act **§2A** directs the Fed to promote "maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates" (A). Conventionally called the *dual mandate* because the third is generally taken to follow from the second. The employment leg was added by the **Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977** and the reporting structure by **Humphrey–Hawkins (1978)**.

"Stable prices" was given a number in **January 2012**: 2% annual change in the PCE price index. Notably, the Fed sets this target itself — Congress did not legislate it, and could change it (A).

**Maximum employment is deliberately not given a number**, because its level is determined by non-monetary factors and shifts over time. The Fed estimates it rather than targets it — a genuine asymmetry in the mandate.

**Framework, as of the research date:** the **August 2025 review** removed flexible average inflation targeting. The "makeup" strategy — deliberately overshooting after undershoots, and the language about inflation "averaging 2 percent over time" — is gone, replaced by flexible inflation targeting with heavier emphasis on keeping expectations anchored ([Fed 2025 Review overview](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/guide-to-changes-in-statement-on-longer-run-goals-monetary-policy-strategy.htm), A). The 2% goal itself was not reopened. The sequence is worth noting without editorialising: FAIT was adopted in August 2020, immediately preceded the largest inflation in forty years, and was withdrawn five years later.

### 6.2 The modern operating framework: an ample-reserves floor

**Before 2008**, reserves were scarce. The New York Desk conducted small open-market operations daily to adjust their quantity so that the federal funds rate — the rate at which banks lend reserves to one another overnight — traded near target. A few billion dollars of operations moved the rate. This is the mechanism in older textbooks, and it is **no longer how policy works** (A/B).

**After 2008**, reserves are abundant. With trillions in the system, no plausible open-market operation changes their scarcity. So the Fed switched from adjusting quantity to setting **administered rates**:

| Tool | What it is | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **IORB** | Interest on reserve balances, paid to banks | The primary lever. A bank will not lend below what it earns risk-free at the Fed |
| **ON RRP** | Overnight reverse repo facility | A floor for non-banks (money funds, GSEs) that cannot hold reserves; catches money the IORB floor would miss |
| **EFFR** | Effective federal funds rate | The published outcome — a volume-weighted median of actual transactions, not a set rate |
| **SRF** | Standing Repo Facility (standing since July 2021) | Ceiling: lends cash against Treasuries at a set rate, capping repo spikes |
| **Discount window** | Primary, secondary, seasonal credit | Backstop, hobbled by stigma (see §6.5) |

Since Congress authorised interest on reserves in October 2008, the Fed's principal instrument has been an administered rate rather than an operation. **The target range is now a corridor the Fed administers rather than a level it hunts for.**

### 6.3 Transmission: from a decision to your life

The chain, with honest uncertainty at each link (B):

1. **Policy rate → money-market rates.** Near-immediate and tight. IORB moves, repo and funds rates follow within a day.
2. **Money market → the yield curve.** Longer rates reflect the *expected path* plus a term premium, which is why forward guidance matters as much as the current setting, and why long rates sometimes move *opposite* to a policy change.
3. **Rates → bank lending and credit spreads.** Bank funding costs reprice, lending standards tighten, corporate spreads widen. Weeks to months.
4. **Rates → asset prices and the exchange rate.** Discount rates on equities and property change; rate differentials move the dollar. Fast, and the most visible channel.
5. **All of the above → spending, hiring, prices.** Slowest and least reliable. Interest-sensitive sectors go first (housing, autos, capex), labour demand follows, prices last.

Milton Friedman's "long and variable lags" remains the honest summary. Empirical estimates cluster around **6–18 months** for peak effect on output and **12–24 months** on inflation, with wide confidence intervals (B). Everything policymakers do is therefore aimed at a forecast, and §9a takes that forecasting record seriously.

### 6.4 The balance sheet, QE, and what "printing money" means

**Assets:** Treasury securities, agency MBS, repo lending, loans to banks, a small gold-certificate account, and premises.
**Liabilities:** Federal Reserve Notes in circulation, reserve balances held by banks, the Treasury General Account, ON RRP balances, and capital.

**QE, mechanically:** the Fed buys a Treasury or agency MBS from a dealer and credits the dealer's bank with new reserves. The Fed's assets rise; reserves — a Fed liability — rise by the same amount. **No currency is printed.** The seller has swapped a bond for a deposit.

So does the Fed "create money"? Two precise answers, and the confusion between them powers a lot of bad argument:

- **Yes**, it creates *base money* (reserves and notes) by keystroke, without limit, in its own unit of account.
- **No**, it does not create most of what the public calls money. **Broad money — the deposits in your account — is created principally by commercial banks when they make loans.** Reserves do not mechanically "multiply" into loans; the money-multiplier story in older textbooks does not describe a system with abundant reserves and no reserve requirement (reserve requirements were set to **zero in March 2020** and have not returned) (A/B).

QE's effect therefore runs through **portfolio rebalancing and signalling** — removing duration from the market and pushing investors into riskier assets — rather than through a mechanical expansion of lending. That distinction matters for the inequality argument in §9a.

**QT** is the reverse, mostly passive: maturing securities are allowed to run off rather than being reinvested, up to a monthly cap. It shrinks assets and drains reserves.

**As of the research date:** QT **ended 1 December 2025** with the balance sheet at roughly **$6.57tn**, down about $2.4tn from the peak — roughly half the pandemic expansion reversed. Runoff stopped early because repo-market stress and reserve-scarcity signals appeared *before* reserves were confidently "ample" ([SVB](https://www.svb.com/market-insights/us-treasuries/the-federal-reserve-ends-qt-key-market-liquidity-insights/), [Banking Exchange](https://www.bankingexchange.com/news-feed/item/10480-treasury-market-resilience-and-the-early-end-to-balance-sheet-runoff), B). Total assets were about **$6.7tn as of 2026-09-02**, and the Fed now buys enough to match trend growth in reserve demand rather than allowing passive shrinkage. St. Louis Fed economists have suggested ample reserves correspond to roughly **10–12% of nominal GDP** (B).

### 6.5 Lender of last resort

**Bagehot's rule** (*Lombard Street*, 1873, public domain): in a panic, lend **freely**, against **good collateral**, at a **penalty rate**. The Fed's crisis behaviour is best judged against this 150-year-old standard, and it has repeatedly satisfied the first two conditions while abandoning the third.

The **discount window** should be the ordinary channel, but it suffers a well-documented **stigma problem**: borrowing signals weakness, so banks avoid it precisely when they need it. Dodd–Frank's two-year disclosure of borrower identities, intended to improve accountability, arguably deepened the stigma — a genuine trade-off between transparency and function (B). In March 2023 some banks queued for the new BTFP rather than use the window on similar terms.

**§13(3)** permits lending to non-banks in "unusual and exigent circumstances". Used heavily in 2008 (Bear Stearns, AIG, the commercial-paper and money-market facilities) and in 2020. **Dodd–Frank narrowed it**: facilities must now be **broad-based** rather than aimed at a single failing firm, and require **Treasury Secretary approval** (A). The 2020 facilities were structured accordingly and carried CARES Act Treasury equity as a first-loss buffer.

### 6.6 Supervision and regulation

The Fed is one of several US banking supervisors, not the only one:

| Institution type | Primary federal supervisor |
|---|---|
| National banks | OCC |
| State member banks | **Federal Reserve** + state |
| State non-member banks | FDIC + state |
| **Bank holding companies / savings & loan holding companies** | **Federal Reserve** |
| Foreign banking organisations' US operations | **Federal Reserve** |
| Designated systemically important firms | **Federal Reserve** |
| Credit unions | NCUA |

Principal tools: **capital** (Basel III risk-based ratios, the leverage ratio and the enhanced SLR, the G-SIB surcharge), **liquidity** (LCR, NSFR), **stress testing** (CCAR/DFAST, which sets the stress capital buffer), **resolution planning** ("living wills"), and participation in the **FSOC**.

**Current direction of travel (2025–26):** Vice Chair for Supervision **Michelle Bowman** has set out a recalibration across all four pillars of the large-bank capital framework — stress testing, the SLR, Basel III risk-based capital, and the G-SIB surcharge. The enhanced SLR revision, finalised with the OCC and FDIC, is designed to make leverage a genuine backstop rather than a binding constraint on low-risk activity such as holding Treasuries — a change with direct implications for Treasury-market liquidity. Stress-testing changes emphasise scenario transparency and public comment ([Bowman testimony, 2026-06-04](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/testimony/bowman20260604a.htm), A; [ABA](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2026/03/feds-bowman-outlines-proposed-bank-capital-rules/), B).

The debate this reopens is old and unresolved: the 2018 tailoring legislation (S.2155) raised the enhanced-supervision threshold from $50bn to $250bn, and the Fed's **own April 2023 review of SVB** concluded that supervisory tailoring and culture contributed to the failure. Critics read the current recalibration against that finding; supporters argue mis-calibrated capital has its own costs and that SVB was a supervisory-execution failure rather than a rules failure. Both readings are available in the record.

### 6.7 Payments

The Fed is the backbone of US payments, a role that predates and outlasts any monetary-policy debate:

- **Fedwire Funds Service** — real-time gross settlement for large-value payments; trillions daily.
- **Fedwire Securities Service** — issuance, transfer and settlement of Treasury and agency securities.
- **National Settlement Service** — multilateral net settlement for cheque, ACH and card clearinghouses.
- **FedACH** — one of two ACH operators (the private Clearing House runs the other).
- **FedNow** — launched **20 July 2023**, instant settlement 24/7/365; adoption has grown steadily from a small base, and it is an *interbank settlement rail*, not a consumer product and **not** a central bank digital currency.
- **Cash** — the Fed orders notes from the Bureau of Engraving and Printing, distributes them through Reserve Bank cash offices, and destroys unfit notes. *For the physical production process, see* `tasks/16-how-paper-money-is-made/` — not repeated here.

### 6.8 The international dimension

**Standing dollar swap lines** with the ECB, Bank of Japan, Bank of England, Swiss National Bank and Bank of Canada, plus temporary lines extended in crises, let foreign central banks supply dollars to their own banks against their own currency. The **FIMA repo facility** (standing since July 2021) lets foreign official institutions raise dollars against Treasuries rather than selling them into a falling market.

This is where the Fed's reach exceeds its mandate. A large share of global borrowing is dollar-denominated, so FOMC decisions transmit to countries with no vote. The critique — associated most prominently with Raghuram Rajan — is that the Fed optimises for US conditions while setting the effective global financial cycle, and emerging markets absorb the volatility. The counter-argument is that a mandate is a mandate, and that swap lines are precisely the mechanism by which the Fed mitigates the spillover it creates (B).

### 6.9 What the Fed does **not** do

Misattribution is the most common error in public discussion:

- It does **not** set fiscal policy, tax, or spend. It cannot cancel debt or fund programmes.
- It does **not** print banknotes (BEP) or mint coins (US Mint). Both are Treasury bureaus.
- It does **not** set mortgage or credit-card rates. It influences the short rate; lenders price off market rates plus spread.
- It does **not** set the exchange rate. **Currency intervention is the Treasury's call**, executed by the New York Fed as agent.
- It does **not** regulate securities markets (SEC/CFTC) or insure deposits (FDIC).
- It does **not** control the money in your bank account in any direct sense, cannot see your transactions, and has issued no CBDC.

## 7. The episodes

### 7.1 1929–33: the founding failure

Between 1929 and 1933 roughly a third of US banks failed and the money stock fell by about a third. The Fed had been created to prevent exactly this (B).

**Friedman and Schwartz** (*A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960*, 1963) indict the Fed directly: it allowed the money stock to collapse and failed at its lender-of-last-resort function. **Bernanke's** 1983 work adds a credit channel — bank failures destroyed lending relationships and information capital, deepening the contraction beyond the monetary aggregates. **Eichengreen** (*Golden Fetters*, 1992) and **Temin** locate the binding constraint in the gold standard, which limited what any central bank could do without abandoning convertibility — and note that countries which left gold earlier recovered earlier.

These are complements more than rivals, and the synthesis is now broadly shared: the monetary collapse was central, the gold standard constrained the response, and the Fed's institutional design — twelve semi-autonomous banks without a decisive centre — made coherent action nearly impossible. That last point produced the **Banking Act of 1935**, which built the modern centralised Board and FOMC. Bernanke's remark at Friedman's 90th birthday — "You're right, we did it. We're very sorry. But thanks to you, we won't do it again" — is the institution's own verdict (C for the framing, B for the history).

### 7.2 1951: the Accord

From 1942 the Fed pegged Treasury yields to finance the war — 3/8% on bills, 2.5% on long bonds. Monetary policy was subordinated to debt management.

By 1950–51 Korean War inflation made the peg untenable. The Fed wanted out; Truman and the Treasury did not. The confrontation was severe — Truman summoned the entire FOMC to the White House — and ended in the **Treasury–Fed Accord of March 1951**, freeing the Fed to set rates independently of the Treasury's financing needs (B).

This is the true birth of operational independence, and it is worth noting how recent that is: for its first 38 years, the Fed was not independent in the sense now taken for granted. It is also why the 2025–26 pressure episodes are read by historians against a 1951 template rather than a 1913 one.

### 7.3 The Great Inflation, 1965–82

Inflation rose from under 2% in the early 1960s to over 14% by 1980 (B). Competing explanations, all with support:

- **Bad estimates.** Policymakers believed the natural rate of unemployment was far lower than it was, and read supply-driven inflation as demand-driven (Orphanides's real-time-data work, B).
- **Bad theory.** The Phillips curve was treated as a stable, exploitable trade-off. Friedman and Phelps had argued it was not, and were right.
- **Political pressure.** Arthur Burns's Fed eased into the 1972 election; the Nixon tapes contain explicit pressure. Historians differ on how much this determined outcomes versus reinforcing Burns's own views (B/C).
- **Supply shocks.** The 1973 and 1979 oil shocks are real but insufficient — inflation was rising well before 1973.

**Volcker's resolution.** Appointed August 1979, on **6 October 1979** he announced a change in operating procedure: target reserve growth and let the funds rate go where it must. It went above 19%. Two recessions followed; unemployment peaked near 10.8% in late 1982. Volcker was denounced by both parties, and inflation was broken.

The lesson taken away — that credibility is worth more than any single output gap, and is cheaper to keep than to rebuild — is the single most influential idea in modern central banking, and it is audible in the July 2026 dissents, which argued that a hike now would "forestall the need for a steeper and potentially more costly sequence of tightening" ([J.P. Morgan AM](https://am.jpmorgan.com/gr/en/asset-management/institutional/insights/portfolio-insights/fixed-income/fixed-income-perspectives/fomc-statement-july-2026/), B).

### 7.4 The Great Moderation, 1983–2006

Inflation low and stable; recessions shallow and rare. Greenspan's reputation was built here, and the period's own self-understanding — that better monetary policy had tamed the cycle — was only partly right. Research since attributes a substantial share to good luck: benign supply conditions, the disinflationary effect of China's integration into world trade, and the absence of large shocks (B).

The lasting critique is the **"Greenspan put"**: interventions in 1987, 1998 and 2001 taught markets that the Fed would cushion severe declines, encouraging risk-taking. Note the trap — this is unfalsifiable in a sense, since a central bank that lets a crisis run to prove it has no put is doing something obviously worse. The moral-hazard problem is real *and* has no clean solution, which is why it recurs in §9a rather than being resolved here.

### 7.5 2007–2010

Compressed, because the literature is enormous:

- **March 2008**, Bear Stearns: a §13(3) facility supports a JPMorgan acquisition. The Fed takes credit risk on a securities firm for the first time since the 1930s.
- **September 2008**, Lehman: no rescue. The Fed's stated reason was the absence of sufficient collateral to lend against lawfully; critics argue the constraint was political. The scholarly debate is unresolved and probably unresolvable (B/C).
- **September 2008**, AIG: an $85bn credit facility days later — the contrast that fuels the inconsistency critique.
- **Facilities**: TAF, PDCF, TSLF, AMLF, CPFF, MMIFF, TALF. Alphabet soup, all under §13(3).
- **December 2008**: rates to 0–0.25%; QE1 begins.
- **QE2 (2010), QE3 (2012–14)**: balance sheet to ~$4.5tn.
- **July 2010**: Dodd–Frank — the Vice Chair for Supervision, the §13(3) narrowing, FSOC, stress tests, and the §1109 one-time GAO audit.

The verdict, roughly shared: the interventions prevented a repeat of 1931–33, and they did so in ways that were legally strained, distributionally regressive, and corrosive of public trust. Both halves belong in any honest account.

### 7.6 March 2020

The fastest, broadest intervention in Fed history. In the "dash for cash", even the Treasury market — the deepest market in the world — stopped functioning as dealers hit balance-sheet limits.

The response: rates to zero (15 March); **unlimited** Treasury and agency MBS purchases (23 March); a full suite of §13(3) facilities including, for the first time, corporate credit (PMCCF, SMCCF), mid-size business lending (MSLP), and municipal debt (MLF), backed by CARES Act Treasury equity; reserve requirements cut to **zero**; swap lines extended.

**The genuinely interesting result: very little of the announced capacity was drawn.** The corporate facilities bought a small fraction of authorised amounts; the Main Street programme lent a small share of its capacity; the municipal facility had two borrowers. The *announcement* restored market function — an extreme demonstration of the credibility channel. It also sharpens the moral-hazard question, because the Fed established that it will backstop corporate credit, and that expectation now exists whether or not it is ever used again (B).

### 7.7 2021–2023: the inflation the Fed did not forecast

Inflation rose through 2021 and was characterised as "transitory". It was not. By June 2022 CPI exceeded 9%. Why the call was wrong, per the subsequent literature (B):

- Supply-chain disruption was read as temporary when its persistence was serially underestimated.
- Fiscal transfers of unprecedented size were treated as a smaller demand impulse than they proved.
- Labour-supply contraction (retirements, immigration, caregiving) was not anticipated.
- The **FAIT framework itself** created a bias toward patience — it had been designed for a decade of undershooting, and it arrived at the moment that decade ended. This is the strongest institutional criticism of the 2020 framework, and it is essentially what the 2025 review conceded by removing the makeup strategy.

Then the reversal: from March 2022, 0–0.25% to 5.25–5.50% by July 2023 — the fastest cycle since Volcker. QT began June 2022.

### 7.8 March 2023: SVB

Silicon Valley Bank failed on **10 March 2023**, Signature on the 12th, First Republic on **1 May**. The mechanism was textbook interest-rate risk: deposits — overwhelmingly uninsured and concentrated in one industry — funded long-duration securities that lost value as rates rose. A run made the unrealised losses real.

The Fed's **own review** (Vice Chair for Supervision Barr, **28 April 2023**) is unusually candid, and it is a primary source worth reading in full. Its findings: supervisors identified the problems but did not act with sufficient force or speed; the 2019 tailoring framework weakened standards for a firm of SVB's size and growth trajectory; and a culture shift toward higher evidentiary burdens before acting slowed intervention.

Response: a systemic-risk exception to protect uninsured depositors, and the **Bank Term Funding Program** — lending against collateral valued at **par** rather than market value, precisely the mark-to-market losses that caused the failure. Bagehot would have recognised the "lend freely"; he would have queried the collateral valuation and the absence of a penalty rate. BTFP closed to new loans on **11 March 2024**, after arbitrage emerged when its rate fell below what banks could earn on the resulting reserves — a design flaw that is instructive about facility pricing.

### 7.9 2024 to the research date

- **August 2025**: framework review concludes; FAIT replaced by flexible inflation targeting ([Fed](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/guide-to-changes-in-statement-on-longer-run-goals-monetary-policy-strategy.htm), A).
- **1 December 2025**: QT ends at roughly **$6.57tn**, halted early on repo-market stress (B).
- **13 May 2026**: **Kevin Warsh confirmed** as chair, 54–45, largely on party lines; sworn in **22 May**. Powell remains a governor with about two years left in that term ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/13/kevin-warsh-wins-senate-confirmation-as-the-next-federal-reserve-chair.html), [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/5/22/kevin-warsh-sworn-in-as-new-us-fed-chair), B).
- **29 June 2026**: ***Trump v. Cook*** — the Supreme Court declines to permit Governor Lisa Cook's removal, holding the President likely to lose because for-cause removal under the Act requires notice and an opportunity to respond. Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch and Barrett dissented. The merits continue below ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/29/supreme-court-lisa-cook-trump-federal-reserve.html), [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/supreme-court-federal-reserve-lisa-cook-firing/), B).
- **28–29 July 2026**: FOMC holds at **3.50–3.75%**, **9–3**, with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan dissenting in favour of a **25bp increase**. The minutes (released 19 August) record officials seeing a need to hike if inflation does not cool ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/19/fed-minutes-july-2026-officials-saw-need-for-rate-hike-if-inflation-doesnt-cool.html), B).
- **Data at the research date**: CPI **3.4% y/y in July 2026** (second consecutive monthly deceleration), core CPI **2.5%**; PCE **~3.67%** and core PCE **~3.29%** for June; unemployment **4.1%**, with participation slipping to **61.4%** — the rate fell partly because the labour force shrank ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/united-states/inflation-cpi), B). August CPI was scheduled for release on **11 September 2026**, the research date, and is **not** reflected here.
- **Balance sheet ~$6.7tn as of 2026-09-02**, up ~$135bn year-on-year — consistent with growth to match reserve demand rather than passive runoff (A via search summary).

The situation as of the research date is therefore genuinely unusual and worth stating without spin: **a new chair, inflation above target for an extended period, a labour market softening through supply rather than demand, a hawkish dissent bloc pushing for hikes, and an unresolved constitutional question about whether governors can be removed at will.**

Second, the Fed's **regional, bank-subscribed structure is unique**, an artefact of the 1913 compromise that no other country copied, and it is the origin of both the "privately owned" confusion and the genuine Reserve-Bank-governance critique.

## 9. Contested ground

### 9a. Serious critiques

**1. Distributional effects of QE.** *The case:* QE works through asset prices. Assets are held disproportionately by the wealthy. Therefore a decade of QE mechanically widened wealth inequality while wage gains lagged. *The counter:* the employment recovery QE supported disproportionately benefits lower-income workers, who suffer most in recessions; the BoE's own analysis found the wealth effect concentrated among existing asset-holders but the alternative — a deeper recession — worse for the bottom of the distribution; and the *flow* measure (income inequality) behaved differently from the *stock* measure (wealth inequality). *Assessment:* the wealth-share effect is well documented; the counterfactual is what is contested, and it is genuinely hard to evaluate (B).

**2. Moral hazard and the "Fed put".** Repeated rescues — 1987, 1998, 2008, 2020, 2023 — teach markets that severe downside will be cushioned, encouraging the risk-taking that makes the next rescue necessary. The strongest version notes that the 2020 corporate-credit facilities extended the put beyond banks to corporate borrowers. *The counter:* letting a financial system fail to teach a lesson imposes the cost on people who took no risk at all, and the 1930s showed what that costs. *Assessment:* unresolved and probably unresolvable within the current framework. It is the strongest structural criticism in this section.

**3. Independence versus democratic accountability.** *The case:* monetary policy has large distributional consequences, and those choices are made by officials — including five voting Reserve Bank presidents — who are not elected and, in the presidents' case, not even Senate-confirmed. *The counter:* the empirical literature linking central-bank independence to lower and more stable inflation is among the more robust findings in macroeconomics, and the mechanism is well understood — governments face an incentive to inflate before elections. *Assessment:* the 2026 *Cook* litigation is this argument moving from seminar to courtroom, and it is not over.

**4. Mandate creep.** Emergency facilities that allocate credit to particular sectors are quasi-fiscal — they decide who gets funded, which is a legislature's job. MBS purchases specifically subsidised housing credit over other uses. Climate scenario analysis drew the same objection. *The counter:* in a crisis someone must act at speed, and the alternative is nothing. *Assessment:* legitimate, and the Fed has itself retreated from the broader end of it.

**5. The forecasting record.** The SEP and staff forecasts missed the 2021–22 inflation badly, and the "transitory" characterisation was a significant error. Evaluations find Fed forecasts are competitive with private forecasters and no better — which is a problem for an institution whose policy must act on forecasts 12–24 months ahead (B). *In fairness:* nobody else forecast it either, and the dot plot is explicitly not a commitment. *Assessment:* real, and an argument for humility in forward guidance rather than for a different institution.

**6. Capture and the revolving door.** Class A directors are bankers with a formal role in the governance of their supervisor. The supervisory workforce moves between the Fed and the banks it supervises. The 2021 trading scandal — two Reserve Bank presidents resigned over personal securities transactions during the 2020 interventions — was a genuine integrity failure, addressed by considerably tighter investment rules in February 2022. *Assessment:* the strongest institutional-design critique in this list, and the one with the clearest available fixes.

**7. The Austrian critique** (Mises, Hayek, Rothbard). Central banking distorts the interest rate — the price coordinating saving and investment across time — and thereby causes the business cycle rather than mitigating it. Artificially low rates misdirect capital into unsustainable projects; the bust is the necessary correction, and preventing it prolongs the misallocation. Rothbard's *The Case Against the Fed* (1994) goes further, arguing fractional-reserve banking with a lender of last resort is inherently fraudulent. *The counter:* the pre-Fed era it implicitly idealises had more frequent and severe panics, and the 1930s liquidationist experiment produced 25% unemployment. *Assessment:* internally coherent, empirically contested, and its historical baseline is its weakest point.

**8. The market-monetarist critique** (Sumner and others). The Fed targets the wrong variable. Nominal GDP level targeting would automatically accommodate supply shocks (which the Fed shouldn't respond to) while fully offsetting demand shocks (which it should), and a *level* target self-corrects past misses. On this view 2008 was a *tight*-money failure — the Fed allowed nominal spending to collapse — which inverts the conventional account.

**9. The MMT critique.** A currency-issuing government is not revenue-constrained; the binding constraint is real resources and inflation. On this view interest-rate policy is a weak and perversely distributive tool (rate rises pay interest to bondholders), and stabilisation should run through fiscal policy and automatic stabilisers — with the central bank's role reduced to holding rates low and supporting Treasury issuance. *The counter:* mainstream economists argue this understates political-economy constraints on discretionary fiscal policy and the inflation risk of fiscal dominance. The 2021–23 inflation, following very large fiscal transfers, is read by both camps as supporting their position.

**10. Rules versus discretion.** A Taylor-type rule is transparent, predictable and testable; discretion invites time-inconsistency and political pressure. *The counter:* every simple rule would have given demonstrably bad advice at some point — including during 2008 and 2020 — and no rule handles structural breaks. The compromise most central banks reached is *constrained discretion*: a fixed target, rule-informed reasoning, and published rationale.

| **"The dollar has lost ~96% of its value since 1913 — proof the Fed failed."** | The price-level arithmetic is roughly right; a 1913 dollar buys a small fraction of what it did. **The inference is the contested part.** The Fed does not target a stable price *level*; it targets 2% annual inflation, so a large cumulative rise is the stated policy working, not failing. Pre-1913, the price level was more stable over long spans but far more volatile year to year, with severe deflations. And the comparison ignores that wages, incomes and returns are also nominal — real US per-capita income rose several-fold over the same period. | BLS CPI series; Historical Statistics of the United States | Whether 2% is the right target, and whether the burden falls unevenly on savers and wage-earners, are legitimate questions — and were partly the subject of the 2025 framework review. |

## 10. Open institutional questions

1. **Where does the balance sheet settle?** QT stopped early because reserve scarcity appeared sooner than expected. If ample reserves are ~10–12% of GDP, the floor system requires a structurally large balance sheet permanently — and every subsequent crisis ratchets it up. No agreed endpoint exists.
2. **Does the floor system survive?** It is operationally robust and politically exposed: paying banks interest on reserves is easy to attack and hard to explain. A return to scarce reserves would be a major operational change with no constituency at present.
3. **Is the 2025 framework the last word?** Flexible inflation targeting with anchored expectations is a return to pre-2020 practice. Whether it handles a genuine supply shock better than FAIT did is untested, and the current episode may be the test.
4. **Removal power.** *Trump v. Cook* preserved Cook's position on due-process grounds without deciding whether Fed governors are constitutionally protected from at-will removal. Four justices dissented. The merits remain open, and the answer determines what independence actually means in law rather than convention.
5. **Treasury-market resilience.** The 2020 dysfunction and the 2019 and 2025 repo episodes point at the same structural issue: dealer balance-sheet capacity has not grown with the debt stock. The SLR recalibration is one answer; whether it is sufficient is unknown.
6. **CBDC.** The Fed has not issued one and the political environment in the US has turned decisively against a retail CBDC, while other jurisdictions advance (digital euro, e-CNY). FedNow is an instant *settlement rail*, not a CBDC — a distinction routinely collapsed in public debate. See `tasks/22-blockchain-smart-contract-market/` for the broader digital-asset landscape.
7. **Stablecoins and private money.** Dollar-denominated stablecoins are a modern form of private money issued against reserve assets — structurally reminiscent of Free Banking era notes, including the discount-from-par risk when the backing is doubted. Whether issuers gain access to Fed accounts is one of the more consequential unresolved questions in US monetary plumbing.
8. **Deferred asset politics.** If remittances do not resume until the 2030s, the Fed will spend a decade explaining why it owes the Treasury nothing while paying banks interest. That is a political vulnerability regardless of the accounting logic.

## 11. How to follow the Fed yourself

The genuinely useful part of this report: everything below is free and primary.

| Source | What it is | What it is good for |
|---|---|---|
| **FOMC calendar** | Eight scheduled meetings a year | Know the dates; everything else keys off them |
| **FOMC statement** | Released 14:00 ET on decision day | **Read it as a diff.** Compare word-for-word against the prior statement — changed adjectives about inflation and the labour market are the signal |
| **SEP / "dot plot"** | Quarterly (March, June, September, December) | Each dot is one participant's *projection*, not a commitment or a vote; the **median** is the headline; the dispersion is the information |
| **Minutes** | Three weeks after each meeting | Where the argument is; count how many participants held each view ("several", "some", "a few" are semi-formal quantifiers) |
| **Transcripts** | Full verbatim, **five-year lag** | The real deliberation. The 2008 and 2020 transcripts are the best available record of crisis decision-making |
| **H.4.1** | Weekly, Thursdays | The balance sheet: assets, reserves, notes in circulation, TGA, deferred asset |
| **H.8** | Weekly | Commercial bank assets and liabilities — lending conditions in near-real time |
| **H.6** | Monthly | Money stock measures |
| **G.19** | Monthly | Consumer credit — a useful early stress indicator |
| **SLOOS** | Quarterly | Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey: banks' own reports of tightening standards. An underrated leading indicator |
| **Beige Book** | Eight times a year | Anecdotal district-level conditions. Qualitative, early, sometimes ahead of the data |
| **Monetary Policy Report** | Semiannual, with testimony | The official narrative, and the hearing that follows |
| **FRED** (St. Louis Fed) | ~800,000 series, free | The single most useful public economic database in the world |
| **FRASER** (St. Louis Fed) | Digitised historical archive | Original 1913 documents, historical statistics, Federal Reserve Bulletins |
| **Reserve Bank research** | Working papers, notes | New York (markets and plumbing), St. Louis (data and monetarist perspective), San Francisco (labour), Cleveland (inflation expectations and nowcasts) |

**Three practical habits.** Read the statement as a diff, not as prose. Treat the dot plot as a distribution, never as a forecast. And when a headline says "the Fed said", check whether it was the FOMC statement (the Committee speaking), the minutes (a summary of views), or one official's speech (one person's opinion) — those are three very different things and are constantly conflated.

## 12. Reading list

### History
| Work | Notes |

## 13. Glossary

**§13(3)** — emergency lending authority for "unusual and exigent circumstances"; post-2010 requires broad-based programmes and Treasury approval. • **§2A** — the mandate section. • **Ample reserves** — a reserve quantity large enough that small changes don't move the policy rate. • **BTFP** — Bank Term Funding Program (2023–24), lending against par-valued collateral. • **Basel III** — international capital and liquidity standards. • **CCAR/DFAST** — annual supervisory stress tests. • **Deferred asset** — accumulated Fed operating losses that must be earned back before remittances resume. • **Discount window** — standing collateralised lending to banks (primary/secondary/seasonal). • **Dual mandate** — maximum employment and stable prices. • **EFFR** — effective federal funds rate; a volume-weighted median of actual trades. • **FAIT** — flexible average inflation targeting (2020–2025, now withdrawn). • **FedNow** — instant interbank settlement service, launched 2023. • **Fedwire** — real-time gross settlement for large-value payments. • **FIMA repo** — facility letting foreign official institutions borrow dollars against Treasuries. • **FOMC** — Federal Open Market Committee; 12 voters. • **FSOC** — Financial Stability Oversight Council. • **G-SIB surcharge** — extra capital for global systemically important banks. • **H.4.1** — weekly balance-sheet release. • **IORB** — interest on reserve balances; the primary policy lever. • **LCR** — liquidity coverage ratio. • **ON RRP** — overnight reverse repurchase facility; the floor for non-banks. • **Open market operations** — purchases/sales of securities under §14. • **PCE** — personal consumption expenditures price index; the target measure. • **QE / QT** — large-scale asset purchases / their unwind. • **Repo** — sale of a security with an agreement to repurchase; collateralised borrowing. • **Reserve balances** — commercial banks' deposits at the Fed; the settlement asset. • **SEP** — Summary of Economic Projections, source of the dot plot. • **SLOOS** — Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey. • **SLR** — supplementary leverage ratio; a non-risk-weighted capital backstop. • **SOFR** — Secured Overnight Financing Rate; the repo-based benchmark that replaced LIBOR. • **SRF** — Standing Repo Facility; the ceiling tool. • **Stress capital buffer** — capital requirement set by stress-test results. • **Swap lines** — reciprocal currency arrangements with foreign central banks. • **TGA** — Treasury General Account, the government's account at the Fed. • **Tailoring** — calibrating rules to bank size and complexity (S.2155, 2018).
