# Task: The Newest UFO/UAP Release — Deep Analysis

Write the result to `output.md` in this folder.

## Objective

Find **the newest significant UFO / UAP (Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena) release** available as of the date the research is performed, and produce a **deep analytical dossier on it** — not a news roundup.

"Release" means a discrete, dated, citable artifact that entered the public record. Any of these qualify:

- An official government or military report, dataset, or case resolution (U.S. DoD **AARO** annual or interim reports, FOIA productions, declassified document tranches, Inspector General reports).
- Congressional or parliamentary action: a hearing with its transcript/video, testimony submitted for the record, a bill or report language on UAP.
- A foreign-government release (UK, France/**GEIPAN**, Japan, Brazil, Chile, Canada, Ukraine, etc.).
- A peer-reviewed paper, preprint, or institutional scientific report (**Galileo Project**, NASA, university or observatory groups).
- A major media release that itself puts *new primary material* into the public record — a documentary releasing unseen footage, a book publishing new documents, a long-form investigation obtaining new records.

A rumour, a social-media post, a podcast appearance with no new artifact, or an unsourced "leak" is **not** a release. If the most-discussed item of the moment is one of those, say so explicitly and analyze the newest *actual* release instead.

## Selecting the subject

1. Determine today's date and state it.
2. Sweep for candidate releases, newest first. Build a short candidate list (5–10 items) with dates.
3. Pick **one primary subject**: the newest item that is both *genuinely new* and *substantive enough to analyze in depth*. Recency alone does not win — a trivial release from yesterday loses to a major report from three weeks ago. **State the selection criteria and justify the choice in one short paragraph**, and list the runners-up in a table with one line each on why they were not chosen.
4. If two releases are close in weight, pick one as primary and treat the other as a secondary subject at roughly a third of the depth.

## Steps of work

1. **Establish the date** and the candidate list, as above.
2. **Obtain the primary source itself.** Download or read the actual report, transcript, paper, or document set — not a news summary of it. If the primary artifact is paywalled, unavailable, or does not exist in retrievable form, say so plainly and work from the best available secondary sources, labelled as such.
3. **Read it closely.** Note structure, scope, methodology, sample sizes, definitions, date ranges, caveats, and — critically — what it declines to say.
4. **Triangulate.** Find at least three independent treatments of the release (official, scientific, journalistic, skeptical) and note where they disagree.
5. **Check the lineage.** Trace what this release supersedes, confirms, contradicts, or quietly drops relative to prior releases in the same series or on the same case.
6. **Analyze** along every layer in the required structure.
7. **Cite** every factual claim with a source link and a publication date.

## Required structure of `output.md`

- **Title + scope line** — the subject release, its date, and the date the research was performed.
- **TL;DR** — 4–6 bullets: what was released, by whom, what is actually new in it, and what it does and does not establish.
- **Subject selection** — the candidate table (Item | Date | Type | Why / why not chosen) and the justification paragraph.
- **What the release actually is** — provenance, publisher, authority, format, length, scope, and the exact date it entered the public record. Link the primary artifact.
- **Contents walkthrough** — the substance, section by section or claim by claim. Quote sparingly and exactly; attribute every quote.
- **Methodology audit** — how the conclusions were reached: data sources, sensor types, sample size, selection and reporting bias, definitions used, statistical treatment, what counts as "resolved". Name the weakest link in the chain.
- **Evidence grading** — table: Claim | Evidence offered | Evidence type (sensor / document / testimony / inference) | Independently corroborated? (Yes / Partial / No) | Confidence (High / Medium / Low).
- **What is genuinely new vs. recycled** — explicit split. Much "new" UAP material is re-packaged; separate the two and say which is which.
- **What the release does not say** — omissions, carve-outs, classification gaps, questions not asked, and scope limits the document states about itself. Distinguish *stated* limits from *apparent* ones.
- **Competing interpretations** — the strongest prosaic reading and the strongest anomalous reading of the same material, each stated fairly and at its best, each with the evidence it rests on and the weakness it has to carry. Then your own assessment of which the evidence better supports, and how confident that is.
- **Context & lineage** — what came before, what this changes, where it sits in the arc of the file.
- **Incentives & actors** — who benefits from this framing, who pushed for the release, who resisted it, what institutional pressures shaped it. Analytical, not conspiratorial: assertions here need evidence like any other.
- **Reception** — how officials, scientists, journalists, and skeptics received it, with representative sourced reactions and where they diverge.
- **Falsifiable implications** — if the anomalous reading is right, what observable should follow? If the prosaic reading is right, what should? Name concrete checks.
- **What to watch next** — pending deadlines, scheduled hearings, promised follow-ups, FOIA suits in progress, each with a date and source.
- **Sources** — consolidated list, each tagged with publication date and source type, primary sources marked.

## Rules & cautions

- **Accuracy over completeness.** Only include items verifiable against a real, dated source. Do **not** invent reports, hearings, papers, titles, names, dates, quotes, or document numbers.
- If web access is unavailable or no qualifying release can be verified, say so explicitly: list the exact queries attempted, what each returned, and report "No verifiable qualifying release found" rather than inventing content. **A short, honest output is a correct output.**
- **Deep analysis is not speculation.** Depth comes from reading the primary artifact carefully and reasoning about what it supports — not from stacking inference on inference. Every analytical claim must be traceable to something cited.
- An official body *investigating* a claim is not that body *confirming* it. A document *existing* is not its contents *being true*. Never blur either.
- Label clearly: (a) documented official action, (b) peer-reviewed finding, (c) individual claim or testimony, (d) speculation.
- Treat single-source, uncorroborated, and anonymously-sourced claims as such, in the text and in the evidence table.
- Where a story rests on a video, image, or document of unestablished provenance, say so every time it is relied on.
- **Steelman both directions.** A report that only debunks, or only credits, has failed this task. State plainly where the evidence is genuinely insufficient to decide.
- Prefer primary sources over aggregators. Avoid social media as sole sourcing for any factual claim.

## Style

- Neutral and analytical — no sensationalism, no wink-and-nudge framing, and equally no reflexive dismissal. Report what is documented and say plainly what is unknown.
- ~1500–2500 words plus tables and the sources list. Depth is the point; padding is not.
- Use clear section headings matching the structure above.
